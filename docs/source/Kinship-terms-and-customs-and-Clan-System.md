# Kinship terms and customs and Clan System

#### Kinship terms and customs and Clan System

Kinship terms are presented in this section because they are used in a clan to show the relation ship among clansmen. “In order to understand totem as a relative, one has to understand the concept of kinship. In most of the African ethnic groups, kinship can be gained either by blood or by marriage or by a ritual. Every birth and every marriage extend the web of kinship” (Ki gen, 2018, p.69). Oshiwambo kinship terms are similar in all different Oshiwambo sub-ethnic groups such as Aandonga, Aakwambi, Aangandjera, Aakwaluuudhi, Aakolonkadhi. However, the terms used in Oshimbadja, Oshimbaanhu, and Oshikwanyama appears to be slightly different from the one used in other dialects, thus terms presented in this subsection exclude these three dialects as they will be presented in the next subsection.

The Oshiwambo words for a relative or kin are omukwetu, omukwazimo or omupambele, but a distinction is made between aakwetu yomekoto (close relatives), aakwetu yokoontayi (distant relatives) and aakwetu yahaniwa (fictive relatives). Aakwetu yomekoto includes the mother’s sisters and brothers, mother‘s sisters’ children, daughter’s children, and the mother’s mother. Aakwetu yokoontayi includes distant maternal cousins, distant maternal aunts, distant maternal uncles, distant maternal nephews and nieces, and aakwetu yahaniwa includes those people who belong to one’s clan but their common ancestor is not known.

These are the people who might have been incorporated into one’s clan but their clan membership is questionable. Aakwetu yokoondungu may also be used to describe distant relatives. This phrase means our kinsmen on the periphery. It must be pointed out, however, the descriptive terms yahaniwa, yonkoontayi, and yokoondungu are often left out in conversation because they are construed as derogatory terms and politically incorrect.

According to Turvey (1977) the word omukwao (his /her relative), is also used to describe one of the ”family circle’, viz. cousin or sibling. “The same word may also mean the stomach, as well as the actual kitchen area, in Oshindonga” (Kuusi 1974, p. 168). Although the word aakwetu (plural of omukwetu) refers to “uterine relative, it may also mean friend, fellow, and companion, neighbour or partner” (Tirronen, 1986, p.170). Furthermore, the word onku (strip to be worked at in the field) or oludhi (clan) is also used to refer to a lineage. Another word that may be used to describe kinship is oludhi which means type,

kind or clan. One can, therefore, say to another person: Nduno oludhi lwandje ndu (this is my type, i.e. this is a member of my clan or lineage). The word oludhi may also mean “race, category, type, gender and sex.” (Tirronen, 1986, p.17). The word ehe (afterbirth) is used to refer to one’s biological child or one’s maternal nieces or nephews. It appears that when one says to another person: ndyono ehe lyandje, it means I am the progenitor of that person. Afterbirth is a synonym of the placenta. As nouns the difference between afterbirth and placenta is that afterbirth is the placenta and other material expelled via the birth canal following childbirth or parturition in mammals. Afterbirth comes from a female. Since ehe comes from a female, it is used when the mother refers to her offspring. The word ehe may be used to replace the word omumwandje (my child). The word ehe may also be used when one refers to one’s place of birth, for example, when one says: Ehe lyandje oli li kOshigambo (Oshigambo is my place of birth).

The word ombinzi (blood) is also used to describe kinship. This word may be used by members of the father’s lineage when they address the father’s children. The same word may be used in denying the paternity of a child. A father and his relatives may say: Ndjono kayi shi ombinzi yetu ndjo (That is not our blood, i.e. We did not father that child).

The word ongudhi (pillar, support) is used to refer to a senior person in the clan or lineage. This is usually a living person to whom a member of the clan may go to for expert advice. Often, this person is the oldest in the lineage who is regarded as an apical clansperson. He or she does not have to be affluent. When this person passes on, it may be said: Ongudhi ya teka po (The pillar is broken). This word, however, may also refer to the husband or resourceful person in the clan.

#### Terms of affinity and consanguinity among Aawambo

In Oshiwambo, the eldest daughter or son is called osheeli. The second-born or next to the youngest child is called ontowele (onhowele). The third child of four or five children, any child between the second and the second last child among six or more children is referred to as omushakati. The word Omushakati means ‘the one in the middle’. The last born is called onkelo. A husband is called omusamane. An elderly married man is called omushungu. An elderly woman who has given birth to one or more children is called omuvalakadhi. By contrast, a marriageable young man, bachelor or young husband is referred to as omushungumati. A wife or married woman, though she may not have given birth is called omukulukadhi. In ancient times, when polygamy was rife, the main wife was referred to as munyalombe/omuku lukadhi gwambanda (senior wife), derived from the words nya and elombe. The former is an archaic form of the Protowambo word na which means to have. The latter means kitchen; hence munyalombe means head of the

main kitchen. The other wives with portfolios are: Gwokatikegumbo (the second wife who is in charge of managing and opening (matula) iigandhi, mahangu storage baskets), mweehaka (the wife in charge of milk and milk products and is responsible for distributing milk among the co-wives), totende (the wife who cracks nuts for marula oil and cooks for the dogs), mutyakemo (the wife who receives guests). Generally, any wife other than munyalombe,mweehaka and gwokatikegumbo may be referred to as omukulukadhi gwokehale (an associate or by-wife).

#### Mother’s kinship terms

Fig 1: Mother’s kinship

Kinship terms express the relationship and obligations towards one another. The kinship relations are guided by the use of kinship terms within the clan system. One’s father is called tate and one’s mother is called meme. The mother’s sister is also called meme and all relatives of mother’s generation, regardless of sex are called oomeme. One’s mother’s brother is called kuku or tatekulu or meme. The mother’s brother may be referred to as meme, because he is related to one through the mother. She has also the same obligations towards ego as ego’s biological mother, for example, traditionally the mother’s brother has the obligation to contribute towards the payment of the fine if ego is fined for an offense. It has to be noted, however, that the word kuku, tatekulu or meme applies to all male relatives of one’s mother of the same gen eration as one’s mother. One’s brother or sister is called omumwameme, the stem – mwa- refers to the relationship while meme refers to mother thus showing matrilineality. Omumwameme may also refer to all maternal cousins. In today’s language, it may also be used to refer to paternal cousins. One’s mother’s brother’s child

is called gwaantsezi (male cross-cousin) or gwontsiyi (female cross-cousin). The word ntsezi means bull because it is believed that your ntsezi can marry you as cross-cousin marriage is allowed. In Oshingandjera, Oshikolonkadhi, and Oshikwaluudhi, the word omukula is used to express similar relations among kinsmen. According to Tirronen (1986), gwaantsezi is the son of father’s sister while gwontsiyi is the daughter of one’s father’s sister. One’s mother’s father is called kuku or tatekulu and one’s mother’s mother is called kuku or meekulu. One’s sister’s child is called omumwandjegona (little son or daughter) just as their children may refer to one’s mother’s sister as memegona. The morpheme – gona means little. In other words, memegona means little mother. Many Aawambo, however, do not like the morpheme – gona because it does not demonstrate full kinship. In Oshikwanyama, for example, the diminutive morpheme is avoided at all costs and the mother’s sister is simply referred to as meme omumwaina wameme (my mother who is the sister of my mother). One’s sister’s son or daughter is called omutekulu. Similarly, one’s daughter’s son or daughter is called omutekulu. In the case of a man this word may also be used to refer to one sister’s child and all the children of one’s parallel and cross cousins. The etymology of the stem – tekulu, is not well known, but it is speculated to have come from the verb stem tekula which means to rear, care for, tend, or bring up. This may be ascribed to the mutual and reciprocal obligations which exist between a grandparent and omutekulu, between the mother’s brother and omutekulu. The omutekulu is expected to be cared for by the mother’s brother, or mother’s parent. Omutekulu is expected to reciprocate when the tatekulu and meekulu are in need. The love between grandparent and okatekulu is said to be greater than the love between the parent and a child. This is summed up in Oshiwambo apothegm: Okatekulu oke vule omwana uukali (A grandchild is greater to stay with than with one’s child, i.e. the love for okatekulu is greater than the love for one’s child). The word omutekulu shifts to okatekulu to express endearment. The nominal prefix –ka- is semantically used in Bantu languages for a similar purpose. The child of one’s granddaughter or grandson is called omutekulululwa.

#### Father’s kinship terms

One’s father is called tate and one’s mother is called meme. The father’s sister may also be called tate/tatekadhi and all relatives of mother’s generation, regardless of sex are called oomeme(plural) meme-(singular). One’s mother’s brother is called kuku or tatekulu or meme. The father’s brother may be referred to as tate because he is related to one through the father. He has also the same obligations towards ego as ego’s biological father, for example, traditionally the father’s brother has the obligation to do the mapping of the homestead for his son. It has to be noted, however, that the word tate applies to all male relatives of one’s father of the same generation as one’s father. One’s brother or sister is called omumwameme, the stem– mwa- refers to the relationship while meme refers to mother thus showing matrilineality. Omumwameme may also refer to all paternal cousins. One’s father’s brother’s child is called mumwaameme (brother/sister). One’s father’s father is called kuku or tatekulu and one’s father’s mother is called kuku or meekulu. One’s brother’s child is called omumwandjegona (little son or daughter) just as their children may refer to one’s father’s brother as tategona. The morpheme – gona means little. In other words, the word tategona means a little father. Many Aawambo, however, do not like the morpheme – gona because it does not demonstrate full kinship. In Oshikwanyama, for example, the diminutive morpheme is avoided at all costs and the father’s brother is simply referred to as tate omumwaina watate (my father who is the brother of my father).

One’s mother-in-law is called kukumweno. One’s father-in-law is also called kukumweno. In some dialects other than Oshindonga, the word meekulu and tatekulu may be used to refer to father-in-law and mother-in-law respectively. One’s mother-in-law is also called meemweno and one’s father-in-law is also called tatemweno. One’s brother-in-law or sister-in-law is referred to as suwala or uukwe. The relationship between one’s brother or sister-in-law is cordial and warm. They are regarded as members of one’s clan therefore it is said: Suwala ezimo (One’s brother or sister-in-law is a member of his/her family in other words, they assist one another as if they belong to the same clan. One also advises his/her sister-in-law to be economically prudent or to be a miser. This is summed in the proverb: Uukwe tu lye ombuku, onyama yongombe katu yi mono. (Brother or sister-in-law let us eat the mouse, we may not find the beef). Eating mouse is equated with indigence and low self-esteem in Oshiwambo culture. Aali yoombuku (mouse eaters are regarded as destitute and imbecile people). In this case one advises his/her brother or sister-in- law to live within his/her economic means. One’s son or daughter- in- law is called oshitenya. One’s co-sister or brother-in-law is called uutenyuukwetu. One’s stepchild is called omumwandje. One’s stepfather is called tate and one’s stepmother is called meme. The use of meme and tate in this context is summed in the proverb: A hokana nyoko oye owala ho (Whoever marries your mother becomes your father). The wife of one’s mother’ s brother is called kuku. The child of your husband’s sister is called omutekulu. The child of your wife’s sister or brother is called omumwandje. The child of one’s wife’s sister’s daughter or son is called omutekulu.

As adumbrated earlier, one’s sister’s husband or one’s brother’s wife is called suwala or uukwe. The word suwala comes from the Afrikaans word swaer. Although this is a sex gender term in Afrikaans which refers to men only, in Oshiwambo it has transformed, thus it refers to both male and female. The word uukwe is a truncated form of the word uukwetu which Tirronen (1986) defines as family circle or kinship. One’s daughter’s husband or son’s wife is called oshitenya. The stem – tenya refers to the daytime. One may be referred to as oshitenya when he/she is formally introduced to the future in- laws, but the official title is bestowed upon him/her after marriage. Traditionally, before a person is not pronounced as oshitenya, he/she does not enjoy freedom in the presence of his ‘future in-laws’.

#### Aakwanyama and Ovambadja kinship terms

Omukwanyama uses the word tate (plural ootate) to refer to his/her father. The word tate may also be used as a title for men, by their position, age, and/or marital status, for example, a teacher, an elderly man or a married man. It can also be used as an opening formula to self- praise- usually used after sneezing, stumbling or in the course of self- praising. In this case, the person would say tatei instead of tate, as a call or invitation to his/her father to intervene. Tirronen (1986) explains that tatei means “catch me”, but the morpheme ‘i’, when suffixed to a name or title expresses a call or cry for help. This comes from the belief that the father is a source of inspiration for his child. Tate may also be used as an interjection or expression of surprise. A child will address any male parent as tate because childrearing is a collective responsibility of all members of the community. Any tate has thus the right to discipline any child from his community.

When referring to another person’s father, he/she uses the word xo (second person) or xe (third person). Xo (plural ooxo) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while xe (plural ooxe) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. In formal settings, a father’s elder brother may be called omukulu watate and the father’s younger brother may be called okandenge katate (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu watate and ondenge yatate can collectively be referred to as tate mumwaina watate. These terms may also be used to refer to the father’s sister and all maternal relatives of the father’s generation. In this context, a woman of his generation can be referred to as tate.

The word tatekulu (plural ootatekulu), on the other hand, is used to refer to the father’s father or the mother’s father or the mother’s brother or all the parallel and cross cousins of the father’s father and mother’s mother. Similarly, when referring to another person’s father’s father, Omukwanyama uses the word xokulu (second person) or xekulu (third

person). Xokulu (plural ooxokulu) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while xekulu (plural ooxekulu) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. Furthermore, a father’s father may be called tatekulu xe yatate, while a mother’s father may be called tatekulu xe yameme. A great grandfather from both maternal and paternal side is addressed as tatekulululwa (also used to address male ancestors or forbears). A man or woman considers tatekulu (a mother’s brother) his or her best friend. A boy or a girl constantly makes small gifts to his or her tatekulu and expects to inherit from him. A tatekulu may always, moreover, be dependent on his nephew or niece in times of need.

However, the word tatekulu can also be used as title or word to show respect to a person by their position in society, for example, a young man who becomes a cabinet minister or counselor or school principal would qualify to be addressed as tatekulu. Ironically, the words tate and tatekulu may be used in soothing or encouraging male children.

Omukwanyama would usually use the word tatemweno to refer to his/her father-in-law. The morpheme -mweno comes from the noun okumwena (to be silent). This implies that a daughter or son-in-law is expected to say very little when conversing with his/ her father-in-law. In most cases, he/she is expected to be silent. For example, during the formal introduction to the father- in-law to be, the prospective daughter or son-in- law is expected to be as brief as possible, using one word or one phrase responses. But this trend continues even after marriage. Nevertheless, this should not be perceived with negative intentions or as a violation of freedom of speech, rather it is designed to avert any potential disagreements that may snowball into conflict.

Omumwatate is used to refer to people born of the same father, but not the same mother. When referring to another person’s half-brother or half-sister, one uses the word omumwaxo (second person) or omumwaxe (third person). Omumwaxo (plural ovamwaxo) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while omumwaxe (plural ovamumwaxe) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. However, there is a shift in the use of this term, because many people feel that it does not express the proximity, thus it is often substituted by the term omwameme.

Omukwanyama uses the word meme (plural oomeme) to refer to his/her mother. The word meme may also be used as a title for women, by their position, age, and/or marital status, for example, a teacher, an elderly woman or a married woman. When used multiple times (memememememememe!), it expresses astonishment, provocation, anger. However, when used with the suffix ‘i’, i.e. memei, it expresses fear and sadness, for instance when a woman comes across a snake. Similarly, in mourning times, it is common for people

to shout out memei. A variation is sometimes meme! or meme yange! used to express surprise, fear, anger, and provocation. A child will address any female parent as meme because childrearing is a collective responsibility of all members of the community. Any meme has thus the right to care for any child from her community.

When referring to another person’s mother, he/she uses the word nyoko (second person) or ina (third person). Nyoko (plural oonyoko) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while ina (plural ooina) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. It must be noted, however, that the word nyoko (and its plural form) is often considered derogatory, even though it is even used in the Bible, for example in the fourth commandment, fimaneka xo nanyoko… [honour your father and mother]. Despite the apparent opposition to the use of nyoko, it is common among adults to use it when addressing children or their juniors, though the former often feel offended. In some cases, children may give a negative response to a question in which the word nyoko is used. For example, when an adult visitor asks for the mother but uses the nyoko, the children’s response might be “she is not around”:

Adult: Nyoko omo e li? [Is your mother in?]

Children: Aae ke mo. [No she is not around.] or Aae, nyoko ke mo, omu na owala meme

[No, your mother is not in, only our mother is in.] [sic]

Children would react in this manner because they feel insulted and expect an adult visitor to use the word meme. By contrast, children may never use the word nyoko when addressing adults.

The derogatory connotations associated with the word nyoko could be linked to the abuse of the word nyoko and the fact that it is used as a swear word, such as in shanyoko ove [sic]. This is one of the worst swear words in Oshiwambo and is very provocative. An insult using this term often results in a physical confrontation. In Oshiwambo, as is the case in other African cultures, private parts of a human body are used in swearing, but it becomes very provocative when the word nyoko is attached, for example, wa tya e*** lya fa lyanyoko. [you have *** similar to your mother’s]. Although to a lesser degree, the same is also true for the use of the word xo.

In formal settings, a mother’s elder brother or sister may be called omukulu wameme and the mother’s younger brother or sister may be called okandenge kameme (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu wameme and ondenge yameme can collectively be referred to as meme

mumwaina wameme. These terms may also be used to refer to the mother’s brother and all maternal relatives of the mother of her generation. In this context, a man can be referred to as meme.

The word meekulu (plural oomeekulu), on the other hand, is used to refer to the father’s mother or the mother’s mother or all the parallel and cross cousins of the mother’s mother. When referring to another person’s mother’s mother, Omukwanyama uses the word nyokokulu (second person) or inakulu (third person). Nyokokulu (plural oonyokokulu) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while inakulu (plural ooinakulu) expresses remoteness- distance between addresser and addressee. Furthermore, a father’s mother may be called meekulu ina yatate, while a mother’s mother may be called meekulu ina yameme. A great grandmother from both maternal and paternal side is addressed as meekulululwa (also used to address female ancestors or forbears).

However, the word meekulu can also be used as title or word to show respect to a person by their position in society, for example, a young woman who becomes a cabinet minister or councilor or school principal would qualify to be addressed as meekulu. Ironically, the words meme and meekulu may be used in soothing or encouraging female children.

Omukwanyama would usually use the word meemweno to refer to his/her mother-in-law. As explained earlier, the morpheme mweno comes from the noun okumwena (to be silent). This implies that a daughter or son-in-law is expected to say very little when conversing with his/her mother-in-law. In most cases, he/she is expected to be silent. For example, during the formal introduction to the father-in-law to be, the prospective daughter or son- in-law is expected to be as brief as possible, using one word or one phrase responses. But this trend continues even after marriage. Nevertheless, this should not be perceived with negative intentions or as a violation of freedom of speech, rather it is designed to avert any potential disagreements that may snowball into conflict.

Both the son and daughter-in-law, call their father-in-law tatemweno. The parents’-in-law refer to both son and daughter-in-law as oshitenya. There is, however, a pattern of avoidance, known as okutila, which means ‘to be afraid.’ The behaviour between the parents’ in-law and the daughter or son in-law is more one of restraint, mutual respect, and refined etiquette than one of actual avoidance. However, Ovakwanyama mother in-law taboo is an avoidance relationship. A man never sits on the ground with his mother-in-law or eats with her. He averts his eyes in shame when speaking to her. A young married woman is bashful in the presence of her husband’s father; a young married man, in the presence of his wife’s mother. A man also casts down his eyes when talking to his future father-in-law (Loeb, 1962).

According to Loeb (1962), mumwameme (plural ovamwameme) is used to refer to a person’s mother’s offspring, her sister’s children and her maternal cousin’s children, including both distant and immediate. The word mumwameme is also used to express friendship. Estermann (1976) confirms that:

…among the Ambo (sic) people, as everywhere, it is customary to employ kinship terms to express a relationship of familiarity, even when no consanguinity exists. In the well-known fable of the hyena and the jackal, of which various episodes are told, the hyena is addressed by the jackal as shimbungu shatate (hyena [relative] of my father) and the jackal is addressed as kavandje kameme (jackal [relative] of my mother). In the course of the narrative, the hyena is familiarly addressed as mukulu wange (my elder sibling) and answered in kind by the jackal, who addresses him as ondenge yange (my younger sibling). It remains to be noted that all the adult members of the royal family arrogated to themselves the right to be called tate or meme by all their subjects, and allowed them no other form of address. Tatekulu was the most commonly used term of address for the reigning chief (p. 90).

Matrilineality is reflected in the word mumwameme, derived from meme. Further evidence of this consanguineous relationship is found when referring to another person’s sibling, Omukwanyama uses the word mumwanyoko (second person) or mumwaina (third person). Mumwanyoko (plural ovamwanyoko) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while mumwaina (plural ovamwaina) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. In formal settings, a person’s elder brother or sister may be called omukulu wange, while their younger brother or sister may be called okandenge kange (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu wange and ondenge yange can collectively be referred to as ovamwameme. Kwanyama brothers, sisters and parallel cousins are very close to one another. It is considered inappropriate for a man to engage in sexual conversations in front of his female vamwaina. Ovakwanyama maintain relationships between cousins, both parallel and cross cousins. For example, the ceremonial relationship between parallel cousins, the children of two brothers, is expressed in the heart feast (omitima), one of the great feasts of Ovakwanyama.

It is the term omutekulu that is used to refer to the equivalent of nephew, niece, grandson or granddaughter. In the first-person singular, one would say omutekulu wange (my nephew, niece or grandchild), but when referring to another person’s nephew, niece, grandson or granddaughter, one uses the word omutekulu woye (second person) or omutekulu waye (third person). A man refers to a sister’s child as omutekulu, but refers to his brother’s child

as omumwange (my child). A distinction is made between the maternal omutekulu and paternal omutekulu. As Loeb (1962) concludes, there is omutekulu wokepata (maternal) and omutekulu wokolupale (paternal). To illustrate this, the following scenario elucidates the affinity:

Hamunyela has two female cousins, Ndahambelela and Nelao. Ndahambelela is the daughter of Hamunyela’s maternal aunt. Nelao is the daughter of Hamunyela’s paternal aunt. Ndahambelela has a son, Nghaamwa, while Nelao has a son, Nghifikepunye. Therefore, Nghaamwa is Hamunyela’s omutekulu wokepata (maternal) and Nghifikepunye is Hamunyela’s omutekulu wokolupale (paternal).

Nonetheless, biological parents do not observe this distinction when it involves the sons and daughters of their offspring. Rather, they may refer to them as ovatekulu vomekoto. “The offspring belonged to the side of the mother and, as a result, it was monitored by their mother’s brother whose kinship title was tatekulu womekoto [the uncle who was cut from the same belly button! umbilical cord as my mother]. The children of his sister were called oimhumba yomekoto” [children of his sister who was cut from the same umbilical cord as himself] (Shemuvalula 1982, p. 20). Women were considered as the right people for expanding their mothers’ clans, whereas men expanded their wives’ mothers’ clans. By extension, the children of one’s children’s children are referred to as ovatekulululwa (singular omutekulululwa) vomekoto.

Cross cousins are referred to as mhedi. Omukwanyama would refer to their father’s sister’s children as mhedi. This is linked to the fact that one can marry the child of his father’s sister or his mother’s brother. But, this is not possible among parallel cousins, who address each other as mumwameme. In other words, children who are born to the same sisters or brothers may not be married to each other. According to Loeb (1962), marriage among cross cousins was beginning to be considered to be on the wane. It is further explained that cross-cousin marriage too, which is dependent on clan structure is now exceptional and is not regarded with favour, although it is common for a man to bundle with his father’s sister’s daughter.

These marriages did exist, though in ancient times. In modern times, marriage among cross cousins is rare. Such marriages sometimes lead to disagreements within family members. This is attributable to the current trend where cross cousins regard one another as mumwameme. According to Loeb (1962), although Ovakwanyama do not approve of cross-cousin marriages, traces of the custom remain in speech and action. For instance, customs, proverbs and related terms appear to indicate that paternal cross-cousin marriage was preferred to maternal cross-cousin marriage; the Oshikwanyama proverb: Kaxo ke dule kanyoko (Your father is more important than your mother) may have been originally an expression of this preference. It was common practice, moreover, for two cross cousins of opposite sexes to joke with each other and call each other bull. A man will say ‘oh, you

are my bull, now we will get married’. The relationship between a youth and his father’s sister’s daughter is intimately connected with the moon. The moon commonly is called ohani, but it (and moonlight) may be called omwedi.

Sckär (as cited in Loeb, 1962) narrates that everyone prays for favours from the new moon (okamwedjona, little bull-woman, or oahani kovalodi, little moon of witches). A person will pray for wealth, cattle or love. If one wishes to harm an enemy, one might also pray for this favour. The full moon which brings the fulfillment of wishes is celebrated with much more dancing and merrymaking.

In Loeb’s (1962) view, if a man’s father’s sister’s daughter (mhedi) sees the new moon first, before the man sees it, she has the right to demand from him anything she wishes and at the full moon, he must oblige her. He cannot refuse any request she makes because when she wishes for the moon, she uses the same word as when she asks for her bull. If she is in love with him, she will ask him to sleep with her; or if she has not gone through efundula, to bundle with her.

Every clan had its eedyaalelidalo, oohekulu yedimo [clan fathers who were its uncles and leaders]. As among the Herero, the Omukwanyama clan fathers had “power of life and death over members of his clan” (Loeb 1962, p.286). “No blood revenge follows” if by chance an uncle killed his nephew while disciplining him (Gibson as cited in Loeb 1962, p.288). Together the uncles had the final authority of directing the youths of their clan on the clan members they could marry and those they could not. Luttig writes: “A maternal uncle protects his nephews and nieces from the fathers’ harshness and they might seek asylum at his kraal” (as cited in Loeb,1962, p.288).

Shemuvalula (1982, p.20) explains that:

It is obvious that not most of the uncles applied harsh disciplinary measures to their nephews and nieces. Among them was a leading clan father who kept the clan wealth and distributed the property of the deceased in the clan. This clan leader was called Nakakoma. The criteria for his promotion were omaongelwandunge [experience], common sense, trust, oungudya [expertise in managing wealth] (Nditya and Ndadilepo 1999). He could assign someone he trusted to do the distribution on his behalf. Ookaukongholwa [dissidents] in clans did not comply with this order, and they often drove the cattle of the deceased from the cattle posts and hid them before they came to the mourning rituals. Nonetheless, it was taboo in some clans to drive the cattle of the deceased. Dissidents were often the deceased’s sisters’ sons. Other members of the clan were the youths who belonged to the side of the mothers. Each clan often had ovakwao vomombiya, hano

vehe fi vetimba [relatives who had joined the clan and thus did not inherit property or participated in any payment for an offense against the clan]. This practice, although not common, is still observable in some communities.

In modern times, with the advent of Christianity and western laws, the practice of inheritance of property has changed. The children of the deceased father and surviving spouse are now the main beneficiaries in the estate of the deceased. This change is often responsible for numerous feuds between clans. The deceased’s clan still believes that they are entitled to the property of their relative. This is especially common in cases where the deceased was opulent.

#### 5.3. 9 The role of a clan father

A clan leader/father is a person who leads the clan and makes all important decisions on behalf of the clan. He serves as the chief advisor for the clansmen and often has the final say in matters about the entire clan. The clan leader has special privileges, access to restricted facilities and a whole host of respectful clansmen under his or her direction. The clan leader must be the guiding light for the clan, looking after internal problems and solving social ills, and he should make it his duty to represent his clan’s view (Wordpress, 2011).

The qualities of a clan leader mentioned by Wordpress agree with the qualities of onkungulu yezimo in the Oshiwambo tradition. This suggests that these appear to be common qualities of a clan leader in various clans of the world. Examples of similar qualities of a clan leader were mentioned in Chapter 4.

Amakali (1995, p.34), explains the role and qualities of the clan leader among Aandonga:

A clan leader who was rich and owned a large herd of cattle and a large number of mahangu storage baskets (iigandhi), was expected to make a significant contribution towards the settlement of the debt. Such a person could rescue many clan members from dying of hunger as he possessed enough food. If he died, then the clan members held a meeting to elect his successor who should possess the following qualities: a miser, economical person, wealthy, wise and diligent. The elected person inherits the clan wealth and must be prepared to come to the aid of the entire clan, should it experience difficulties.

Every clan has its oohekulu yezimo [clan fathers who are its uncles and leaders]. As among Ovaherero, Ovakwanyama and Aandonga, clan fathers had “power of life and death over members of his clan” (Loeb 1962, p. 286). A leading clan father keeps the

clan’s wealth and distributes the property of the deceased in the clan. This clan leader is called Onkungulu. The criteria for his promotion are owino (experience), common sense, trust, uungundja [expertise in managing wealth]. He can assign someone he trusts to do the distribution on his behalf. Niikondo (2018) explains that onkungulu yezimo is the pillar of the clan whose role is to unite it. Niikondo further claims old people in their 60s and 70s are heads of the clans and are highly respected.

Like a king, a clan father was fortified to enable him to carry out his functions properly. Maria, a daughter of a well-known medicine man, Hamutoko, said that a clan father oha tumbikwa [is made stronger mentally and physically]. Both Maria and Nditya confirmed etumbiko [the empowering] but could not remember the rite or the medicine used for this purpose. The noun okutumbikwa means to prepare someone, especially a young person, to be stronger mentally and physically. The claws of either a vulture or an eagle were put on sinews around the neck of a young boy or a puppy (Nditya and Hamutoko, as cited in Ndamanomhata, 2001). The idea behind this was to make the boy or a puppy eagle-eyed; it was the wish that the positive char acteristics of seeing very far be transferred either to a boy or a puppy. The word is also used when, for instance, young persons are trained to carry out tasks that are beyond their natural possibilities (Ndamanomhata, 2001).

Ndamanomhata (2001) further describes a clan father as follows:

The system of clan fathers as leaders of their clans kept the next of kin together. This had remarkable advantages in traditional Owambo society: the children of sis ters and half-sisters were able to know, and to relate to each other; they were able to be taught which taboos were associated with their clan and which were not. For instance, youngsters were advised from which clan and household they could marry and from which not. Obedience, good manners, hard work, a good upbringing, wealth, status and physical appearance were some of the criteria for choosing a life partner. The sisters and half-sisters of the clan fathers had the significant task of educating their juniors to know their clan members, especially their uncles (p.111).

A clan father is also regarded as shimutsikeni (the one who is burdened with difficulties) or ongudhi yezimo (pillar of the clan). In this case the clan leader is called shimutsikeni because he/she is an arbitrator whose one of his tasks is to settle the conflict within the family. He is expected to carry the difficulties of the entire clan. The word ongudhi yezimo is also used for a similar purpose. Haapanen (1958) has collected a proverb that shows that the clan leader is the pillar of the clan: Omutsa a kwata uuyinga waa tengenye=

Omukuluntu oha koleke ezimo (The poor one holds the small horns intact= The elder makes a clan strong and united).

The role of tategona and memegona

Generally, among the various Aawambo totemic clans, tategona and memegona perform different roles. These roles are performed throughout one’s entire life, from birth to death. Child-rearing is not considered as merely the responsibility of the biological parents, but it extends to the patrilineal and matrilineal relatives of both the mother and the father. The patri lineal and matrilineal responsibilities towards the child are based on the culturally prescribed gender roles.

#### Tate[gona]

Tirronen (1986, p.398) defines “hegona as my /our father’s brother or sister.” This definition requires expansion because uuhegona (the state of being one’s father maternal relative is associated with the task of caring for the son or daughter of one’s male maternal relative).Uuhegona in Oshiwambo is not confined to brothers or sisters but it also applies to the maternal relatives of one’s father, but it is only married women with special qualities who qualify for uuhegona.

Tategona is usually a female relative of the father whose task is to care for a son or daughter of her brother/maternal relative from birth to death. In very rare cases a man may be approached to carry out this task as a tategona, but this is a violation of the traditional norms which regard a woman as a source of fortune (Oye ha tema omulilo gwelago).

Any father selects a hegona or shenkadhi for his child. Such a woman is given the responsibil ity to take care of the child until the child dies- if hegona dies, a maternal relative of hegona is appointed. For one to become a hegona of someone’s child, certain requirements should be met: a) She should be legally married; b) she should have reached menopause; c) she should have a stable marriage; d) she should have a proven record of exemplary behaviour; and e), in today’s context, she must be financially stable. A woman who has abandoned her husband (i.e. divorced or separated) or who mistreats her husband would not qualify to become hegona. It is believed that the abuser of men or the divorcee would contaminate someone’s daughter or son with such bad qualities.

Aawambo believe that an unmarried woman does not have the authority to preside over marriage because she has not gone through the same procedure. Oshiwambo marriage is legalised through the official handing over of omasiga (cooking stands) to the married

woman by her mother-in-law or by hegona. A woman who has not officially been given the omasiga is not considered legally married, according to the Oshiwambo custom. Similarly, a man who has not eaten the sorghum or bran porridge, inaa lya oshithima shiilyawala nenge shuuhutu, is not legally married. Among Ovakwanyama, marriage is also legalised through the offering of fresh mopane sticks to a woman, okundwangulilwa oifonono. It is thus logical that hegona should have been in a stable marriage over a long period, having exercised patience and has gone through the thick and thin of marriage. This is necessary because hegona is considered a chief advisor to the married couple. The parents of the bride and groom believe that hegona would transfer her experience in marriage to the newlyweds. Due to the modern financial demands of weddings, some parents also take into account the financial position of hegona in the hope that she will be able to sponsor the wedding (i.e. with cash, wedding materials or a cow).

The roles of tategona are very specific. At the birth of a child, the mother and the baby spend a few days in seclusion. After the umbilical cord has fallen off, the mother and the baby are released from seclusion by hegona of the child. Hegona introduces the baby to its future gender roles i.e. the baby boy is introduced to cattle herding and the baby girl is introduced to either the cooking or pounding area. The official name of the baby born to an unmarried father is bestowed upon a baby by its hegona. Similarly, the first born baby (osheeli) of a married man is given a name by its hegona who often is its paternal grandmother. This explains why it is common, among the Aawambo, that most of the sheeli are named after a relative of the father. On the occasion the baby receives an official name, it receives a traditional necklace (ondigu), a baby carrier (ondhikwa) and a belt, for a boy, or omuhanga (traditional beads) for a girl from the paternal clan. Ondigu is very significant, in that it is passed on from one child to the next (from osheeli to ontowele and so on) in a married family. However, the necklace of the twins is not passed on, because twins are special babies whose mother undergoes cleansing. Children born out of wedlock or before marriages are treated differently (i.e. each receives a different ondigu). Ondhikwa is considered sacrosanct because a mother may never leave it behind when she has a baby. Besides, ondhikwa symbolizes the generational existence of a clan, hence the use of phrases, such as ngame ogwondhikwa yangandi [I hail from so and so’s ondhikwa], ke shi gwondhikwa yetu [he or she does not hail from our ondhikwa]. This means that the word ondhikwa can also be used to refer to a lineage.

At baptism, in several ethnic groups of Aawambo, the godparents are also the hegona of the child. According to the Code of Canon Law, “Insofar as possible, one to be baptized is to be given a sponsor who is to assist an adult in Christian initiation, or, together with the parents, to present an infant at the baptism, and who will help the baptized to lead a Christian life in harmony with baptism, and to fulfill faithfully the obligations connected

with it” (No. 872). In other words, godparents share the child-rearing and disciplining with the biological parents. A godparent is also expected to present the child with gifts on a baptismal day, to strengthen the ties between himself and the child’s parents. This is similar to the traditional naming ceremony where the child is presented with traditional gifts by hegona. This justifies the trend among several Aawambo ethnic groups to choose a hegona as a godparent.

This responsibility of hegona continues at the confirmation of a child. The hegona is expected to accompany his godchild to church on the day of confirmation. In the church, he is required to accompany his godchild to the altar, to take Eucharist with his godchild. Besides, hegona usually presents his child with a Bible and a hymn book.

Hegona is expected to perform various activities during the wedding ceremony. On the day the banns are made in the church, hegona moves to the homestead of the groom or bride and lives there until the wedding period is over. During her stay she cares for the bride or groom, for example, she cooks for her daily. She also conducts other activities such as moulding cooking stands, lighting the wedding fire and so on. She communicates with ancestral spirits before conducting any activity. However, due to Christianity, some oohegona avoids communicating with the ancestral spirits. It is important to present the important activities carried out by hegona during the wedding period to give a clear picture of the procedure followed:

First, she smears the bride or groom to be with red ochre mixed with fat. The mixture is applied to the whole body of the bride or groom to be. The bride or groom usually remains dressed in shorts while the hegona does her job. This takes place at the oshoto shondjugo (the reception area opposite the mother’s hut) of hegona. People who have accompanied the bride or groom to be are also slightly smeared with the mixture. After performing this activity, the hegona gives the bride or groom a special gift which could be a goat, chicken or money as present. This is to wish the bride or groom luck to receive more gifts for the wedding. Ovakwanyama observe the same practice. However, the bride or groom is to be blessed by hegona in his or her parents’ homestead. This is usually done at the main olupale (main assembly area) or okashotona (the small assembly area opposite the wife’s hut). In some ethnic groups, Aangandjera and Aakwaluudhi, the bride or the bridegroom receives ondjaya from hegona which he/she wears on the right side whereas the one she receives from the memegona is worn on the left side.

The next day, hegona travels to the house of the bride or groom to prepare omasiga (cooking stands) to be used for the wedding party. Accompanied by several women, in the morning, she collects clay from an anthill and moulds it into cooking stands. Afterward, other women follow suit. The moulding of cooking stands usually takes place in the husband’s section in the homestead. Thereafter, hegona erects a mopane tree in the homestead to wish the bride or groom good luck.

After making the omasiga, she makes the main fire. Everybody who wants to make a fire during the wedding ceremony takes a coal from the main fire to light a new fire. When she makes omasiga or fire, she performs various rituals, such as putting a needle under the fireplace. If for any reason hegona is delayed, on the day of singing, referred to as omaimbilo or eeshungi or eemhoko (the day before the wedding day) and arrives late, the house dwellers may decide to make fire. But as soon as they see her coming, such fire is extinguished so that she makes a new fire. It is believed that the fire made by a person other than hegona is not blessed. Thus, hegona may be referred to as gwombambi (duiker/ buck) or gwosizi (tortoise) since these animals symbolise good luck in Oshiwambo. It has to be noted that these designants may apply to both men and women.

A day before the ceremony hegona brews beer, ta hanga omalovu. She boils the sorghum flour and makes the first beer. Nobody else is allowed to make beer before she is done. She also performs various magics as she makes beer, for example, she stirs the beer with a sorghum stalk and puts beer in her mouth and blows it into the air to wish the wedding good luck and also to give the omalovu to the ancestors to taste.

On the wedding day before the procession enters the homestead, the bride and bridegroom are seated somewhere outside the homestead. Hegona of the bride or bridegroom, depending on where the procession is, moves closer to couple to give the bride and bridegroom the omalovu (beer), she takes a cup and takes the beer out of the pot and pours into the cup until it overflows. The beer that spills is given to the ancestors to taste first. She does it carefully so that the beer does not bubble. If it bubbles, then it is believed that one is swearing at the ancestors who in turn curse the newlywed couple. She gives a cup first to the bridegroom and then to the bride. Both the bride and bridegroom receive the cup with both hands and empty the beer without pausing. As she pours beer into the oshitenga, she calls out the name of the ancestors. For example, at one wedding ceremony in 2017, hegona uttered these words:

Niilwa yAamutenya ileni tu fukalakeni

Marta gaShilongo ileni tu lyeni ohango yokaana ketu. Angula dhaSheya penduka tu tante ohango yokaana ketu

(Niilwa daughter of Sheya come and join us in leading our child to adulthood. Marta daughter of Shilongo, join us in enjoying the food for our child’s wedding. Angula, the son of Sheya wake up and join us in dancing for the celebration of our child’s wedding).

Afterward, hegona takes the baskets and puts in the gifts. Before she does this, she waves efungu (dancing wand) and ululates. Hegona is given special meat at a wedding such as front leg in some ethnic groups, or omapitakati (rib cage) in Ondonga from each ox slaughtered. In some Aawambo ethnic groups for example among the Aakwaluudhi, hegona is given the omugongo (the back part of a slaughtered ox). If hegona does not receive sufficient meat, she becomes angry and leaves the party. If that happens, then she curses the couple to become childless. To appease the anger of hegona the couple travels to the homestead of hegona to tender an apology and to ask her to remove bad luck. They shower her with lots of gifts and help her with house chores for a couple of days. When she is satisfied, she performs magic to purify the couple. Once the couple is cleansed of back luck, hegona will massage the tummy of the wife and say: Ka vale nokomushila (Go and produce more children).

When the couple decides to establish their new homestead, they inform hegona of the husband. Hegona will do the mapping of the new homestead. She gets up at dawn and leads her/his child to the new dwelling. Hegona leads the procession and carries the burning omusindilo (a magic stick). When the procession arrives in the new dwelling, the people put their luggage at olupale where hegona makes the fire to officially inaugurate the new dwelling. The owner of the house and his entourage warm themselves before the fire. After a certain period, hegona comes back to bless the entrance. Again, she performs his/her magic.

In addition to the inauguration of the homestead, when her daughter or son passes on, the hegona is officially informed, because no fire is made before she comes to make the holy fire for the mourning period. If she is delayed, and someone makes the mourning fire before she arrives, then such fire is extinguished as soon as she arrives. She makes the first fire for the mourning session. It is believed that the ash thrown in the omudhime symbolizes that death is reduced within the family. When people walk over the ash from the mourning period, they become contaminated with death, i.e. death will strike their families. In times of death, Shigwedha and Nampala (2006) maintain that the fire was set by a highly trusted individual who knew the family secret, and who was of the paternal clan of the deceased. Worst of it some men appoint their wives as hegona. Some men also appoint themselves as hegona of their sons or daughters. The biological father or mother cannot become hegona. Once a father calls himself a hegona, it means he has relinquished his responsibility as the biological father, because he has regarded himself as the father in a small way. If hegona dies, then it is inherited by her eldest daughter who then acts as a hegona for the man or woman for whom her deceased mother acted as a hegona. In other words, ohanga is transferred from other to her daughter. It would be appropriate to practice uuhegona consistently in accordance with our tradition. However, some Christians claim: Tse Aakriste katu na shidhila/shipwe (We Christians do not stick to taboos). This is said when they want to appoint unqualified people for the role of hegona.

The role of hegona is, however, still significant in the modern world. When one has bought a car, he/she takes it to the homestead of hegona for blessing. The owner of the car informs he gona that he will come to show him his/her new car. On the day set, the owner of the car drives to the homestead of hegona. When the owner of the car arrives, who usually brings along a calabash of beer, he/she moves in the homestead and exchanges greetings with hegona.

Afterward, hegona takes her olutengwa (a container containing fat mixed with ochre), moves with the owner of the car toward the place where the car is parked. Hegona then takes a horsetail waving it around as she dances and ululates while moving around the car inspecting it. Hego na then takes olukula and rubs the entire car with it. Once she is done, she ululates again and praises the owner of the car and the paternal clan of the car owner. The owner of the car is not allowed to wash off olukula. Olukula has to remain on his car until it falls off by itself. It is believed that washing off of olukula means washing off your luck and blessing. Some oohegona today only rub the tires of the car with the olukula. This custom is still common among Aawambo because cars are still seen smeared with olukula. Instead of rubbing the car with olukula, they simply shake hands with the owner of the car and

thank him or her. In modern days, some oohegona use vaseline or body lotion instead of

olukula. Nonetheless, the use of vaseline contradicts Oshiwambo culture.

#### 8.0.0.2 Meme[gona] and Tatekulu

In this case, meme includes both male and female relatives of one’s mother of the same generation as one’s biological mother. The roles of meme are different from those of tategona. While tategona is entrusted with active roles in most ceremonies in one’s life, meme is mostly responsible for settling the debt incurred by a clan member.

Meme or memegona takes care of his /her sisters’s children when a sister passes on or when the children of the sister are in need. She/he can raise the children of her/his sister. In modern time meme can foot the medical bill of his /her sister’s children and pay educational fees.

The maternal clan (ezimo lyokoomeme) also plays a role in the life of a child. If someone commits an offense the whole maternal clan is held responsible for such an offense e.g. If a boy impregnates a girl, then the maternal clan has to raise money to pay for the damage. If a boy absconds or dies, the payment still has to be made because all clan members are pronounced guilty by the girl’s clan. The maternal relatives do not refuse to make their contribution towards a payment because they are often afraid of ontiko (contagious magic

A child can inherit property from its mother and maternal relatives. If a clan practices witchcraft, the ability to practice witchcraft or sorcery is passed on to the children by the mother. If the father is the omulodhi (wizard/ witch), the children would not be considered to have the ability to practice witchcraft but when the mother practices witchcraft; all the children would be regarded as aalodhi (witches/ wizards).

Explaining the role of the maternal clan in paying debts incurred by a clan member, Amakali (1995) narrates as follows:

In the ancient time, when one’s clan member killed a person, it was said that the totemic clan of the murderer stored a person in a hut (okwe mu pungula kondunda). The complaining clan thus requests that their clan member be released from the hut (i.e. a fine must be paid as a sign of atonement). People gave cattle as payment for the offense committed by their clan members. The whole clan of the culprit was held responsible for the transgression and was liable to make the payment collectively. It was said ihadhi olele kezimo, debts do not remain permanently within a clan. Clan leaders held a meeting and resolved to settle the debts(p. 34).

Closely allied to memegona is the tatekulu (mother’s brother). This tatekulu is is also addressed as meme. The role of tatekulu is not as broad as that of tatekulu. Miettinen (2005) argues that a social father is under a rigorous regime of matrilineality, i.e. a maternal uncle was not without importance in the life of the individual among Aawambo. He was a highly respected person, but his role was activated only when something unusual happened. The special respect for a maternal uncle is seen, for example, in the fact that while the father was called tate, maternal uncle was called either tatekulu or kuku, terms indicating that the person addressed is more senior and more respected than mere tate. A person’s maternal uncle was regarded as his or her “best friend,” and a boy was expected to take constant small gifts to his tatekulu.

The uncle’s role is activated if there is a major conflict between the father and his son or daughter, for example. If things go badly, a person could leave his or her father’s homestead and move to live with this uncle, because he was regarded as the protector of his nephews and nieces and was thus obliged to give them shelter in time of need. Shelter is also given when the children’s mother dies, for in such a case the children do not normally remain with their father but instead the guardianship passes to maternal relatives. An uncle also has the right to be involved in cases his nephew committed serious wrongdoings and is expected to punish him, even to kill him if necessary. This is something a biological father is not allowed to do.

However, tatekulu has little influence on the life of his sister’s children in the modern- day, because there is a propensity to promote the nuclear family system. The Maintenance Act (No. 9 of 2003) makes it obligatory for the biological father to support his offspring. Besides, Westernisation and its didactic import have resulted in people abandoning their extended family system, to a certain extent.

In light of the roles of both hegona and memegona, it seems obvious that children belong to the paternal-cum-maternal clan. Nowadays, children can inherit from their fathers. Children can also succeed their father as headmen. Modern laws that make it obligatory for the children to inherit from their fathers have, to a certain extent, forced Aawambo to abandon some of the old practices though not entirely.

The role of epulangunga

Epulangunga is a fearless person within the clan whose task is primarily to demand for the payments of debts especially in case the debtor hesitates to settle his/her debt. It is praised as:

Enima le ku tumwa li velifa Oudu woye nau kalelele

Vakweni nava ufanwe Noshikefa landeni Ongobe yeefya nai uye

(This rascal if sent to collect you

You must pretend to be ill Your illness must be chronic Let your relatives be invited

Let the coffin be bought

Let the ox for mourning period be bought)

This verse refers to the aggressive attitude of epulangunga. It is a merciless character who can easily resort to killing a debtor. Epulangunga threatens the debtor with physical attack and death. The words coffin and mourning ox are mentioned to show that epulangunga resorts to violence which leads to the death of the victim.

Every clan has epulangunga (male person whose main role in the clan or lineage is to demand in name of his relative, the payment of a debt from somebody). Such a male is fearless, merciless and irascible. He is approached by any clan member to confront the debtor. He is praised as Epulangunga, enima lyowala (The one who demands debts, a worthless person). Enima lyowala in this context refers to the state of being inconsiderate because epulangunga is expected to exhibit rough and irrational behaviours when he demands payment of debts. Besides, clansmen appear to have little respect for him, but they rely on him when a debtor shows reluctance.

Furthermore, epulangunga threatens the clan of the debtor with severe punishment if the clan of the debtor fails to pay the debts within the prescribed time. The epulangunga is often armed when he goes to demand the debts from a certain lineage or clan. He can either be accompanied by a clansman or confront the clan of the debtor alone. The merit of uupulangunga is that it often results in the immediate settlement of the debts, but the demerit of this function is that the epulangunga can attack the debtor or any of the debtor’s clansmen, thereby dragging the clan of epulangunga into legal battle. An informant remarked that: “Otali vulu okweeta oshikolo kezimo. Otali vulu oku kwe

etela ashike olya pumbiwa puudhigu nopokuma tapu nyengathana. Otali ku mangulula” (Epulangunga may plunge the clan into trouble. He can land you into trouble but he is needed when difficulties become unbearable and when the im possible comes one’s way. He frees one from trouble).

#### 5.3.12 Clan system and administration of justice

A clan leader advises members of a totemic clan to tell their children to refrain from committing a serious offense that may bring the name of the entire clan into disrepute or which may cause the clan to be fined for the wrongdoing. According to Oshiwambo tradition when a member of the clan commits an offense, all members of that clan are held responsible for the payment of an offense. The guilty person undertakes a trip to see members of his or her clan, telling them that Onde eta oshikolo kezimo (lit. I have brought debt to the clan, i.e. I have committed a serious offense which makes the entire clan liable for payment of the offense I committed). But in most cases, a close relative conveys a message to all clansmen.

Jazzie (2010) reports a similar situation among the Navajo of North America:

The Navajo use k’e (term which means a wide range of deeply felt emotions which create solidarity of the individual with his/her or her clan). The Navajo use k’e to achieve restorative justice where there is a dispute the procedure which we call“talking things out” works like this: Every person concerned or affected by the dispute or problem receives a notice of gathering to talk things out. At the gathering, everyone has the opportunity to be heard. In the vertical legal system, the zone of dispute is defined as being only between the people who are directly involved in the problem. On the other hand, as Navajo, if my relative is hurt that concerns me; if my relative hurts another I am responsible to the injured person (p.343).

This shows that considering an individual member of a clan‘s offense as an offense that direct ly affects the entire clan appears to be a universal phenomenon. As is a case among Aawambo, the Navajo of North America believe that if a member of their clan is injured then the entire clan is injured. If a member of one’s clan hurts another person, then the entire clan is held responsible for the offense thus every clan member attempts to attend the peacemaking gathering to contribute the solution of the problem.

Among Aawambo members of the clan do not refuse to contribute to the settlement of payment as they fear that members of another clan may enlist the service of a traditional healer to curse their clan (ontiko). Loeb (1962), writing on the penalties for assault and murder among Ovakwanyama explains:

When a person killed a member of another clan, revenge was necessary whether the victim was a man, a woman, or a child, and whether the killing was deliberate or unintentional. Only members (ovakwawo) of the slain person’s clan, however, might execute such vengeance legally. The murderer’s clan tried to save his life by hiding him. His family also went into hiding. The matter was an end and the quilt erased only when the victim’s clanmates had killed either the murderer or one of the murderer’s clanmates.

In some instance a compromise was possible. A murderer and his relatives hurried to round up cattle to give to the dead man’s clan. The peace party that brought the gifts place marula nuts (iigongoti) on the ends of their spears to show their intention, i.e. to show that they appeal for forgiveness and refrain from retaliation. When eight or ten head of cattle had been presented to the aggrieved clan, members of this clan re moved the murderer’s intestines and stretched them on the ground. Beside them, the guilty clan must lay out an equal length of ostrich shell beads. They must also give a brick of salt of the same size as the dead’ man’s head. The final step, the payment of a ball of tobacco insured peace. A pipe was passed around and each person took a few puffs. Smoking the pipe of peace was called okunwa kombiya.. (p.71).

Although Loeb describes the situation among Ovakwanyama, this situation appears to be a global trend, as Freud (1919) reports:

The social side of totemism is primarily expressed in a sternly observed command ment and a tremendous restriction. The members of a totem clan are brothers and sisters, pledged to help and protect each other; if a member of the clan is slain by a stranger the whole ethnic group of the slayer must answer for the murder and the clan of the slain man shows its solidarity in the demand for expiation for the blood that has been shed.

Similarly, Miettinen (2005), writing on the clan system of Aandonga, claims that:

The matriclans were also something that might be called units of common legal responsibility and action. Native Commissioner C. H. L. Hahn claimed that in civil cases it was the clans, and not individuals, who were plaintiffs. Court orders which nominally were against individuals were in reality against the whole clan, as the clan was liable for all the actions of its members. The means of dealing with murder is highly illustrative in this respect; murder was normally compensated for with a certain number of cattle forfeited to the matrilineal relatives of the victim, and the murderer’s clan was liable to pay this if he was unable to do so. If no compensation was paid, the relatives of the deceased had the right to exact blood vengeance, which could be directed against a close relative of the murderer, at least in cases where the murderer himself was not available for punishment. Vengeance was not allowed, however, if the murderer’s clan had cleansed the guilt by performing a certain sacrificial rite, or if the king had pardoned the murderer (p.111).

Furthermore, Tönjes (1996) makes similar observations regarding the jailed murderer in Uukwanyama:

When a murderer was apprehended, he was chained and placed in stocks. He could only be released upon payment of a large fine, between eight to ten head of cattle. If he was unable to pay his fine, his clanmates usually made the payment for him. As soon as possible, his relatives brought in a large ox as the first installment, to prevent t his sale or execution. Not infrequently, however, the rich relatives of an unpopular criminal simply left him to his fate.

When the criminal himself could not be caught, the aggrieved clan tried to seize another member of his family- his mother, brother, sister, uncle or aunt. Propitiation was sometimes necessary when a killing occurred during a cattle raid by Omukwanyama upon an Omukwanyama kraal owner, even though the raid had been carried out by the king’s orders. If one of the king’s men was killed, the killer’s clan had to pay (p.72).

Tönjes further narrates that if the thief could not be taken away and when he possessed no assets, his relatives were held reprehensible. If a thief has stolen too many items, and his relatives were unable to pay, then, one of his relatives was sold as a slave into slavery. The thief that was caught was tied up until his relatives paid the compensation to have

him redeemed. The payment of the stolen item was determined according to its value “When the thief dies, before the payment for the stolen property was made, the relatives had to bear the responsibility for the payment.” (Tönjes, 1996, p. 110). There is a saying in Oshiwambo: Oongunga ihadhi olele kezimo (lit. the debts do not get rotten in the clan, i.e. clan members must be prepared to settle the debts at all costs).

When the thief was killed while stealing items from a homestead, his family members feel offended and asked questions which demonstrate that they are not happy with what had happened to their relative, they then demand that the clan of the killer pay for the blood spilled. “This payment includes gild, shells, beads, tobacco, and cattle. After the payment of the fine, the peace pipe was shared and the affair was at the end. When the payment was not made promptly, someone in the killer’s clan might be put to death or some of the clan’s cattle might be seized in retaliation.” (Loeb, 1962,p. 86). Gibson, Larson, and McGurk (1981) observe similar situations among the Sambyu of Kavango. It is clear that “death does not clear the murderer‘s guilt, for a guilty person’s heir not only inherits his assets but his guilt as well. The family of a deceased offender is responsible for paying his fines. If a wronged person dies, his matrilineal relatives or his father takes up the case” (p.146).

This means death is not seen as a way of exonerating the clan members from payment of debts owed to another person by the deceased thus after the corpse is buried, a meeting is held so that the debtor may reveal how much or what the deceased owed him/ her. Even in modern times, a question is asked by the chairperson of the meeting, who is usually the headman or his representative. The chairperson asks, “Opu na ngu wa lilwa sha kunakusa nenge wu na sha we mu pele ndee ine shi galula. onawa wu shi popye mpaka montaneho yaakwazimo (Is there anyone who is owed something by the deceased or who gave the deceased something which he did not return. It is good to say it in the presence of the clan members). If there are people who are owed, then they reveal it so that members of the clan can discuss later how to settle the debts. Besides, if any clan member is told by the deceased that so and so is indebted to the deceased, the clan member informs the gathering that so and so owed the deceased something. The respondent then rises to either defend himself or admits that quilt and explains how he would settle his debts. The respondent cannot appeal for the write off of the debts, because he/she is aware that the relatives of the deceased demand the payment from the clan not necessarily from an individual.

It seems the mutual responsibility of clan members is also a common trend in the first world as Goodwin (2009), writing on American Indian culture, explains:

The clan plays an important role in cases of murder, rape, and injury whether accidental or intentional. Unless settlement was reached through payment, a blood feud resulted between the clans involved. Material payment varied with the degree of harm and the social position of the injured person. Settlement was attempted by tactful members of the offender’s clan, not necessarily blood relatives, accompanying the emissary. After satisfactory payment was made, speakers emphasised that no further bad feeling should exist between clans. The injured individual or close blood kin in his clan might in some cases seek revenge by destroying the property of the offender or even of a member of the latter’s clan if closely related by blood. In atonement payments, those concerned usually were closely related to the defendant by blood as well as clan affiliation (p.402).

This suggests that clansmen would protect and assist one another in times of need. It appears that the relatives of the offender are obliged to pay to avoid being attacked by the clansmen of the complaining clan, thus a clansman who commits an offense for which payment is obligatory approaches his/her clansmen for assistance.

Today among the Aawambo if the family of the offender is not in a good financial standing, his mother may make use of the electronic media to convey a message to the clansmen to assist him/her. A message of this nature was sourced from NBC Oshiwambo radio service:

Furthermore, witchcraft was seen as the practice of a particular family. Tönjes (1996) describes the severity of witchcraft about clan system:

The penalty for causing death through witchcraft was death. The relatives of the slain person were not allowed to mourn. Such order to execute the witch was given by the king. These words signified that the family no longer shared the criminal’s guilt (p.70).

According to the Oshiwambo tradition, a criminal may be handed over to the king to be executed. He/she may also be blacklisted by his/her clan. They regard him/her as shinkilishipanda (an evildoer or black sheep). Such an attitude towards an offender is revealed in the following proverb: Nando wa toola oongombe molundago, wa tondwa kezimo lyoye.

Furthermore, if a pregnant girl had an abortion, her entire clan was held responsible for the payment of the fine known as ofuto yonkwele (fine for abortion). Different clans in ancient times harboured fear for ofuto yonkwele, thus girls and other women were warned to refrain from abortion because committing such an offense meant a clan had to give many cattle as compensation.

The pregnancy of an unmarried woman is considered a shame to the entire clan. A pregnant girl may be regarded as an outcast by her clansmen. Traditionally, any girl that fell pregnant before marriage was burnt to death or subjected to cruel abortion. This situation forced some girls to flee their polity to seek asylum in other oshilongo.

#### Kinship terms and customs

Kinship terms are presented in this section because they are used in a clan to show the relation ship among clansmen. “In order to understand totem as a relative, one has to understand the concept of kinship. In most of the African ethnic groups, kinship can be gained either by blood or by marriage or by a ritual. Every birth and every marriage extend the web of kinship” (Ki gen, 2018, p.69). Oshiwambo kinship terms are similar in all different Oshiwambo sub-ethnic groups such as Aandonga, Aakwambi, Aangandjera, Aakwaluuudhi, Aakolonkadhi. However, the terms used in Oshimbadja, Oshimbaanhu, and Oshikwanyama appears to be slightly different from the one used in other dialects, thus terms presented in this subsection exclude these three dialects as they will be presented in the next subsection.

The Oshiwambo words for a relative or kin are omukwetu, omukwazimo or omupambele, but a distinction is made between aakwetu yomekoto (close relatives), aakwetu yokoontayi (distant relatives) and aakwetu yahaniwa (fictive relatives). Aakwetu yomekoto includes the mother’s sisters and brothers, mother‘s sisters’ children, daughter’s children, and the mother’s mother. Aakwetu yokoontayi includes distant maternal cousins, distant maternal aunts, distant maternal uncles, distant maternal nephews and nieces, and aakwetu yahaniwa includes those people who belong to one’s clan but their common ancestor is not known. Goody (1973) calls this artificial kinship. This happens when a person (or family) migrates to an area occupied by people who do not belong to his clan, “such a person (or family) may establish ties and relationships with people in the vicinity. These ties may become just as strong as if the people were genealogically related” (p. 78).

These are the people who might have been incorporated into one’s clan but their clan membership is questionable. Aakwetu yokoondungu may also be used to describe distant relatives. This phrase means our kinsmen on the periphery. It must be pointed out, however, the descriptive terms yahaniwa, yonkoontayi, and yokoondungu are often left out in conversation because they are construed as derogatory terms and politically incorrect.

According to Turvey (1977) the word omukwao (his /her relative), is also used to describe one of the ”family circle’, viz. cousin or sibling. “The same word may also mean the stomach, as well as the actual kitchen area, in Oshindonga” (Kuusi 1974, p. 168). Although the word aakwetu (plural of omukwetu) refers to “uterine relative, it may also mean friend, fellow, and companion, neighbour or partner” (Tirronen, 1986, p.170). Furthermore, the word onku (strip to be worked at in the field) or oludhi (clan) is also used to refer to a lineage. Another word that may be used to describe kinship is oludhi which means type,

kind or clan. One can, therefore, say to another person: Nduno oludhi lwandje ndu (this is my type, i.e. this is a member of my clan or lineage). The word oludhi may also mean “race, category, type, gender and sex.” (Tirronen, 1986, p.17). The word ehe (afterbirth) is used to refer to one’s biological child or one’s maternal nieces or nephews. It appears that when one says to another person: ndyono ehe lyandje, it means I am the progenitor of that person. Afterbirth is a synonym of the placenta. As nouns the difference between afterbirth and placenta is that afterbirth is the placenta and other material expelled via the birth canal following childbirth or parturition in mammals. Afterbirth comes from a female. Since ehe comes from a female, it is used when the mother refers to her offspring. The word ehe may be used to replace the word omumwandje (my child). The word ehe may also be used when one refers to one’s place of birth, for example, when one says: Ehe lyandje oli li kOshigambo (Oshigambo is my place of birth).

The word ombinzi (blood) is also used to describe kinship. This word may be used by members of the father’s lineage when they address the father’s children. The same word may be used in denying the paternity of a child. A father and his relatives may say: Ndjono kayi shi ombinzi yetu ndjo (That is not our blood, i.e. We did not father that child).

The word ongudhi (pillar, support) is used to refer to a senior person in the clan or lineage. This is usually a living person to whom a member of the clan may go to for expert advice. Often, this person is the oldest in the lineage who is regarded as an apical clansperson. He or she does not have to be affluent. When this person passes on, it may be said: Ongudhi ya teka po (The pillar is broken). This word, however, may also refer to the husband or resourceful person in the clan.

#### Terms of affinity and consanguinity among Aawambo

In Oshiwambo, the eldest daughter or son is called osheeli. The second-born or next to the youngest child is called ontowele (onhowele). The third child of four or five children, any child between the second and the second last child among six or more children is referred to as omushakati. The word Omushakati means ‘the one in the middle’. The last born is called onkelo. A husband is called omusamane. An elderly married man is called omushungu. An elderly woman who has given birth to one or more children is called omuvalakadhi. By contrast, a marriageable young man, bachelor or young husband is referred to as omushungumati. A wife or married woman, though she may not have given birth is called omukulukadhi. In ancient times, when polygamy was rife, the main wife was referred to as munyalombe/omuku lukadhi gwambanda (senior wife), derived from the words nya and elombe. The former is an archaic form of the Protowambo word na which means to have. The latter means kitchen; hence munyalombe means head of the

main kitchen. The other wives with portfolios are: Gwokatikegumbo (the second wife who is in charge of managing and opening (matula) iigandhi, mahangu storage baskets), mweehaka (the wife in charge of milk and milk products and is responsible for distributing milk among the co-wives), totende (the wife who cracks nuts for marula oil and cooks for the dogs), mutyakemo (the wife who receives guests). Generally, any wife other than munyalombe,mweehaka and gwokatikegumbo may be referred to as omukulukadhi gwokehale (an associate or by-wife).

Kinship terms express the relationship and obligations towards one another. The kinship relations are guided by the use of kinship terms within the clan system. One’s father is called tate and one’s mother is called meme. The mother’s sister is also called meme and all relatives of mother’s generation, regardless of sex are called oomeme. One’s mother’s brother is called kuku or tatekulu or meme. The mother’s brother may be referred to as meme, because he is related to one through the mother. She has also the same obligations towards ego as ego’s biological mother, for example, traditionally the mother’s brother has the obligation to contribute towards the payment of the fine if ego is fined for an offense. It has to be noted, however, that the word kuku, tatekulu or meme applies to all male relatives of one’s mother of the same gen eration as one’s mother. One’s brother or sister is called omumwameme, the stem – mwa- refers to the relationship while meme refers to mother thus showing matrilineality. Omumwameme may also refer to all maternal cousins. In today’s language, it may also be used to refer to paternal cousins. One’s mother’s brother’s child

is called gwaantsezi (male cross-cousin) or gwontsiyi (female cross-cousin). The word ntsezi means bull because it is believed that your ntsezi can marry you as cross-cousin marriage is allowed. In Oshingandjera, Oshikolonkadhi, and Oshikwaluudhi, the word omukula is used to express similar relations among kinsmen. According to Tirronen (1986), gwaantsezi is the son of father’s sister while gwontsiyi is the daughter of one’s father’s sister. One’s mother’s father is called kuku or tatekulu and one’s mother’s mother is called kuku or meekulu. One’s sister’s child is called omumwandjegona (little son or daughter) just as their children may refer to one’s mother’s sister as memegona. The morpheme – gona means little. In other words, memegona means little mother. Many Aawambo, however, do not like the morpheme – gona because it does not demonstrate full kinship. In Oshikwanyama, for example, the diminutive morpheme is avoided at all costs and the mother’s sister is simply referred to as meme omumwaina wameme (my mother who is the sister of my mother). One’s sister’s son or daughter is called omutekulu. Similarly, one’s daughter’s son or daughter is called omutekulu. In the case of a man this word may also be used to refer to one sister’s child and all the children of one’s parallel and cross cousins. The etymology of the stem – tekulu, is not well known, but it is speculated to have come from the verb stem tekula which means to rear, care for, tend, or bring up. This may be ascribed to the mutual and reciprocal obligations which exist between a grandparent and omutekulu, between the mother’s brother and omutekulu. The omutekulu is expected to be cared for by the mother’s brother, or mother’s parent. Omutekulu is expected to reciprocate when the tatekulu and meekulu are in need. The love between grandparent and okatekulu is said to be greater than the love between the parent and a child. This is summed up in Oshiwambo apothegm: Okatekulu oke vule omwana uukali (A grandchild is greater to stay with than with one’s child, i.e. the love for okatekulu is greater than the love for one’s child). The word omutekulu shifts to okatekulu to express endearment. The nominal prefix –ka- is semantically used in Bantu languages for a similar purpose. The child of one’s granddaughter or grandson is called omutekulululwa.

#### Father’s kinship terms

One’s father is called tate and one’s mother is called meme. The father’s sister may also be called tate/tatekadhi and all relatives of mother’s generation, regardless of sex are called oomeme(plural) meme-(singular). One’s mother’s brother is called kuku or tatekulu or meme. The father’s brother may be referred to as tate because he is related to one through the father. He has also the same obligations towards ego as ego’s biological father, for example, traditionally the father’s brother has the obligation to do the mapping of the homestead for his son. It has to be noted, however, that the word tate applies to all male relatives of one’s father of the same generation as one’s father. One’s brother or sister is called omumwameme, the stem– mwa- refers to the relationship while meme refers to mother thus showing matrilineality. Omumwameme may also refer to all paternal cousins. One’s father’s brother’s child is called mumwaameme (brother/sister). One’s father’s father is called kuku or tatekulu and one’s father’s mother is called kuku or meekulu. One’s brother’s child is called omumwandjegona (little son or daughter) just as their children may refer to one’s father’s brother as tategona. The morpheme – gona means little. In other words, the word tategona means a little father. Many Aawambo, however, do not like the morpheme – gona because it does not demonstrate full kinship. In Oshikwanyama, for example, the diminutive morpheme is avoided at all costs and the father’s brother is simply referred to as tate omumwaina watate (my father who is the brother of my father).

#### Aakwanyama and Ovambadja kinship terms

Omukwanyama uses the word tate (plural ootate) to refer to his/her father. The word tate may also be used as a title for men, by their position, age, and/or marital status, for example, a teacher, an elderly man or a married man. It can also be used as an opening formula to self- praise- usually used after sneezing, stumbling or in the course of self- praising. In this case, the person would say tatei instead of tate, as a call or invitation to his/her father to intervene. Tirronen (1986) explains that tatei means “catch me”, but the morpheme ‘i’, when suffixed to a name or title expresses a call or cry for help. This comes from the belief that the father is a source of inspiration for his child. Tate may also be used as an interjection or expression of surprise. A child will address any male parent as tate because childrearing is a collective responsibility of all members of the community. Any tate has thus the right to discipline any child from his community.

When referring to another person’s father, he/she uses the word xo (second person) or xe (third person). Xo (plural ooxo) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while xe (plural ooxe) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. In formal settings, a father’s elder brother may be called omukulu watate and the father’s younger brother may be called okandenge katate (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu watate and ondenge yatate can collectively be referred to as tate mumwaina watate. These terms may also be used to refer to the father’s sister and all maternal relatives of the father’s generation. In this context, a woman of his generation can be referred to as tate.

The word tatekulu (plural ootatekulu), on the other hand, is used to refer to the father’s father or the mother’s father or the mother’s brother or all the parallel and cross cousins of the father’s father and mother’s mother. Similarly, when referring to another person’s father’s father, Omukwanyama uses the word xokulu (second person) or xekulu (third

person). Xokulu (plural ooxokulu) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while xekulu (plural ooxekulu) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. Furthermore, a father’s father may be called tatekulu xe yatate, while a mother’s father may be called tatekulu xe yameme. A great grandfather from both maternal and paternal side is addressed as tatekulululwa (also used to address male ancestors or forbears). A man or woman considers tatekulu (a mother’s brother) his or her best friend. A boy or a girl constantly makes small gifts to his or her tatekulu and expects to inherit from him. A tatekulu may always, moreover, be dependent on his nephew or niece in times of need.

However, the word tatekulu can also be used as title or word to show respect to a person by their position in society, for example, a young man who becomes a cabinet minister or counselor or school principal would qualify to be addressed as tatekulu. Ironically, the words tate and tatekulu may be used in soothing or encouraging male children.

Omukwanyama would usually use the word tatemweno to refer to his/her father-in-law. The morpheme -mweno comes from the noun okumwena (to be silent). This implies that a daughter or son-in-law is expected to say very little when conversing with his/ her father-in-law. In most cases, he/she is expected to be silent. For example, during the formal introduction to the father- in-law to be, the prospective daughter or son-in- law is expected to be as brief as possible, using one word or one phrase responses. But this trend continues even after marriage. Nevertheless, this should not be perceived with negative intentions or as a violation of freedom of speech, rather it is designed to avert any potential disagreements that may snowball into conflict.

Omumwatate is used to refer to people born of the same father, but not the same mother. When referring to another person’s half-brother or half-sister, one uses the word omumwaxo (second person) or omumwaxe (third person). Omumwaxo (plural ovamwaxo) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while omumwaxe (plural ovamumwaxe) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. However, there is a shift in the use of this term, because many people feel that it does not express the proximity, thus it is often substituted by the term omwameme.

Omukwanyama uses the word meme (plural oomeme) to refer to his/her mother. The word meme may also be used as a title for women, by their position, age, and/or marital status, for example, a teacher, an elderly woman or a married woman. When used multiple times (memememememememe!), it expresses astonishment, provocation, anger. However, when used with the suffix ‘i’, i.e. memei, it expresses fear and sadness, for instance when a woman comes across a snake. Similarly, in mourning times, it is common for people

to shout out memei. A variation is sometimes meme! or meme yange! used to express surprise, fear, anger, and provocation. A child will address any female parent as meme because childrearing is a collective responsibility of all members of the community. Any meme has thus the right to care for any child from her community.

When referring to another person’s mother, he/she uses the word nyoko (second person) or ina (third person). Nyoko (plural oonyoko) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while ina (plural ooina) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. It must be noted, however, that the word nyoko (and its plural form) is often considered derogatory, even though it is even used in the Bible, for example in the fourth commandment, fimaneka xo nanyoko… [honour your father and mother]. Despite the apparent opposition to the use of nyoko, it is common among adults to use it when addressing children or their juniors, though the former often feel offended. In some cases, children may give a negative response to a question in which the word nyoko is used. For example, when an adult visitor asks for the mother but uses the nyoko, the children’s response might be “she is not around”:

Adult: Nyoko omo e li? [Is your mother in?]

Children: Aae ke mo. [No she is not around.] or Aae, nyoko ke mo, omu na owala meme

[No, your mother is not in, only our mother is in.] [sic]

Children would react in this manner because they feel insulted and expect an adult visitor to use the word meme. By contrast, children may never use the word nyoko when addressing adults.

The derogatory connotations associated with the word nyoko could be linked to the abuse of the word nyoko and the fact that it is used as a swear word, such as in shanyoko ove [sic]. This is one of the worst swear words in Oshiwambo and is very provocative. An insult using this term often results in a physical confrontation. In Oshiwambo, as is the case in other African cultures, private parts of a human body are used in swearing, but it becomes very provocative when the word nyoko is attached, for example, wa tya e*** lya fa lyanyoko. [you have *** similar to your mother’s]. Although to a lesser degree, the same is also true for the use of the word xo.

In formal settings, a mother’s elder brother or sister may be called omukulu wameme and the mother’s younger brother or sister may be called okandenge kameme (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu wameme and ondenge yameme can collectively be referred to as meme

mumwaina wameme. These terms may also be used to refer to the mother’s brother and all maternal relatives of the mother of her generation. In this context, a man can be referred to as meme.

The word meekulu (plural oomeekulu), on the other hand, is used to refer to the father’s mother or the mother’s mother or all the parallel and cross cousins of the mother’s mother. When referring to another person’s mother’s mother, Omukwanyama uses the word nyokokulu (second person) or inakulu (third person). Nyokokulu (plural oonyokokulu) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while inakulu (plural ooinakulu) expresses remoteness- distance between addresser and addressee. Furthermore, a father’s mother may be called meekulu ina yatate, while a mother’s mother may be called meekulu ina yameme. A great grandmother from both maternal and paternal side is addressed as meekulululwa (also used to address female ancestors or forbears).

However, the word meekulu can also be used as title or word to show respect to a person by their position in society, for example, a young woman who becomes a cabinet minister or councilor or school principal would qualify to be addressed as meekulu. Ironically, the words meme and meekulu may be used in soothing or encouraging female children.

Omukwanyama would usually use the word meemweno to refer to his/her mother-in-law. As explained earlier, the morpheme mweno comes from the noun okumwena (to be silent). This implies that a daughter or son-in-law is expected to say very little when conversing with his/her mother-in-law. In most cases, he/she is expected to be silent. For example, during the formal introduction to the father-in-law to be, the prospective daughter or son- in-law is expected to be as brief as possible, using one word or one phrase responses. But this trend continues even after marriage. Nevertheless, this should not be perceived with negative intentions or as a violation of freedom of speech, rather it is designed to avert any potential disagreements that may snowball into conflict.

Both the son and daughter-in-law, according to Bruwer (1966), call their father-in-law tatemweno. The parents’-in-law refer to both son and daughter-in-law as oshitenya. There is, however, a pattern of avoidance, known as okutila, which means ‘to be afraid.’ The behaviour between the parents’ in-law and the daughter or son in-law is more one of restraint, mutual respect, and refined etiquette than one of actual avoidance. However, Ovakwanyama mother in-law taboo is an avoidance relationship. A man never sits on the ground with his mother-in-law or eats with her. He averts his eyes in shame when speaking to her. A young married woman is bashful in the presence of her husband’s father; a young married man, in the presence of his wife’s mother. A man also casts down his eyes when talking to his future father-in-law (Loeb, 1962).

According to Loeb (1962), mumwameme (plural ovamwameme) is used to refer to a person’s mother’s offspring, her sister’s children and her maternal cousin’s children, including both distant and immediate. The word mumwameme is also used to express friendship. Estermann (1976) confirms that:

…among the Ambo (sic) people, as everywhere, it is customary to employ kinship terms to express a relationship of familiarity, even when no consanguinity exists. In the well-known fable of the hyena and the jackal, of which various episodes are told, the hyena is addressed by the jackal as shimbungu shatate (hyena [relative] of my father) and the jackal is addressed as kavandje kameme (jackal [relative] of my mother). In the course of the narrative, the hyena is familiarly addressed as mukulu wange (my elder sibling) and answered in kind by the jackal, who addresses him as ondenge yange (my younger sibling). It remains to be noted that all the adult members of the royal family arrogated to themselves the right to be called tate or meme by all their subjects, and allowed them no other form of address. Tatekulu was the most commonly used term of address for the reigning chief (p. 90).

Matrilineality is reflected in the word mumwameme, derived from meme. Further evidence of this consanguineous relationship is found when referring to another person’s sibling, Omukwanyama uses the word mumwanyoko (second person) or mumwaina (third person). Mumwanyoko (plural ovamwanyoko) expresses proximity between the addresser and the addressee, while mumwaina (plural ovamwaina) expresses remoteness- the distance between addresser and addressee. In formal settings, a person’s elder brother or sister may be called omukulu wange, while their younger brother or sister may be called okandenge kange (Loeb, 1962). Both omukulu wange and ondenge yange can collectively be referred to as ovamwameme. Kwanyama brothers, sisters and parallel cousins are very close to one another. It is considered inappropriate for a man to engage in sexual conversations in front of his female vamwaina. Ovakwanyama maintain relationships between cousins, both parallel and cross cousins. For example, the ceremonial relationship between parallel cousins, the children of two brothers, is expressed in the heart feast (omitima), one of the great feasts of Ovakwanyama.

It is the term omutekulu that is used to refer to the equivalent of nephew, niece, grandson or granddaughter. In the first-person singular, one would say omutekulu wange (my nephew, niece or grandchild), but when referring to another person’s nephew, niece, grandson or granddaughter, one uses the word omutekulu woye (second person) or omutekulu waye (third person). A man refers to a sister’s child as omutekulu, but refers to his brother’s child

as omumwange (my child). A distinction is made between the maternal omutekulu and paternal omutekulu. As Loeb (1962) concludes, there is omutekulu wokepata (maternal) and omutekulu wokolupale (paternal). To illustrate this, the following scenario elucidates the affinity:

Hamunyela has two female cousins, Ndahambelela and Nelao. Ndahambelela is the daughter of Hamunyela’s maternal aunt. Nelao is the daughter of Hamunyela’s paternal aunt. Ndahambelela has a son, Nghaamwa, while Nelao has a son, Nghifikepunye. Therefore, Nghaamwa is Hamunyela’s omutekulu wokepata (maternal) and Nghifikepunye is Hamunyela’s omutekulu wokolupale (paternal).

Nonetheless, biological parents do not observe this distinction when it involves the sons and daughters of their offspring. Rather, they may refer to them as ovatekulu vomekoto. “The offspring belonged to the side of the mother and, as a result, it was monitored by their mother’s brother whose kinship title was tatekulu womekoto [the uncle who was cut from the same belly button! umbilical cord as my mother]. The children of his sister were called oimhumba yomekoto” [children of his sister who was cut from the same umbilical cord as himself] (Shemuvalula 1982, p. 20). Women were considered as the right people for expanding their mothers’ clans, whereas men expanded their wives’ mothers’ clans. By extension, the children of one’s children’s children are referred to as ovatekulululwa (singular omutekulululwa) vomekoto.

Cross cousins are referred to as mhedi. Omukwanyama would refer to their father’s sister’s children as mhedi. This is linked to the fact that one can marry the child of his father’s sister or his mother’s brother. But, this is not possible among parallel cousins, who address each other as mumwameme. In other words, children who are born to the same sisters or brothers may not be married to each other. According to Loeb (1962), marriage among cross cousins was beginning to be considered to be on the wane. It is further explained that cross-cousin marriage too, which is dependent on clan structure is now exceptional and is not regarded with favour, although it is common for a man to bundle with his father’s sister’s daughter.

These marriages did exist, though in ancient times. In modern times, marriage among cross cousins is rare. Such marriages sometimes lead to disagreements within family members. This is attributable to the current trend where cross cousins regard one another as mumwameme. According to Loeb (1962), although Ovakwanyama do not approve of cross-cousin marriages, traces of the custom remain in speech and action. For instance, customs, proverbs and related terms appear to indicate that paternal cross-cousin marriage was preferred to maternal cross-cousin marriage; the Oshikwanyama proverb: Kaxo ke dule kanyoko (Your father is more important than your mother) may have been originally an expression of this preference. It was common practice, moreover, for two cross cousins of opposite sexes to joke with each other and call each other bull. A man will say ‘oh, you

are my bull, now we will get married’. The relationship between a youth and his father’s sister’s daughter is intimately connected with the moon. The moon commonly is called ohani, but it (and moonlight) may be called omwedi.

Sckär (as cited in Loeb, 1962) narrates that everyone prays for favours from the new moon (okamwedjona, little bull-woman, or oahani kovalodi, little moon of witches). A person will pray for wealth, cattle or love. If one wishes to harm an enemy, one might also pray for this favour. The full moon which brings the fulfillment of wishes is celebrated with much more dancing and merrymaking.

In Loeb’s (1962) view, if a man’s father’s sister’s daughter (mhedi) sees the new moon first, before the man sees it, she has the right to demand from him anything she wishes and at the full moon, he must oblige her. He cannot refuse any request she makes because when she wishes for the moon, she uses the same word as when she asks for her bull. If she is in love with him, she will ask him to sleep with her; or if she has not gone through efundula, to bundle with her.

Every clan had its eedyaalelidalo, oohekulu yedimo [clan fathers who were its uncles and leaders]. As among the Herero, the Omukwanyama clan fathers had “power of life and death over members of his clan” (Loeb 1962, p.286). “No blood revenge follows” if by chance an uncle killed his nephew while disciplining him (Gibson as cited in Loeb 1962, p.288). Together the uncles had the final authority of directing the youths of their clan on the clan members they could marry and those they could not. Luttig writes: “A maternal uncle protects his nephews and nieces from the fathers’ harshness and they might seek asylum at his kraal” (as cited in Loeb,1962, p.288).

Shemuvalula (1982, p.20) explains that:

It is obvious that not most of the uncles applied harsh disciplinary measures to their nephews and nieces. Among them was a leading clan father who kept the clan wealth and distributed the property of the deceased in the clan. This clan leader was called Nakakoma. The criteria for his promotion were omaongelwandunge [experience], common sense, trust, oungudya [expertise in managing wealth] (Nditya and Ndadilepo 1999). He could assign someone he trusted to do the distribution on his behalf. Ookaukongholwa [dissidents] in clans did not comply with this order, and they often drove the cattle of the deceased from the cattle posts and hid them before they came to the mourning rituals. Nonetheless, it was taboo in some clans to drive the cattle of the deceased. Dissidents were often the deceased’s sisters’ sons. Other members of the clan were the youths who belonged to the side of the mothers. Each clan often had ovakwao vomombiya, hano

vehe fi vetimba [relatives who had joined the clan and thus did not inherit property or participated in any payment for an offense against the clan]. This practice, although not common, is still observable in some communities.

In modern times, with the advent of Christianity and western laws, the practice of inheritance of property has changed. The children of the deceased father and surviving spouse are now the main beneficiaries in the estate of the deceased. This change is often responsible for numerous feuds between clans. The deceased’s clan still believes that they are entitled to the property of their relative. This is especially common in cases where the deceased was opulent.

#### 5.3. 9 The role of a clan father

A clan leader/father is a person who leads the clan and makes all important decisions on behalf of the clan. He serves as the chief advisor for the clansmen and often has the final say in matters about the entire clan. The clan leader has special privileges, access to restricted facilities and a whole host of respectful clansmen under his or her direction. The clan leader must be the guiding light for the clan, looking after internal problems and solving social ills, and he should make it his duty to represent his clan’s view (Wordpress, 2011).

The qualities of a clan leader mentioned by Wordpress agree with the qualities of onkungulu yezimo in the Oshiwambo tradition. This suggests that these appear to be common qualities of a clan leader in various clans of the world. Examples of similar qualities of a clan leader were mentioned in Chapter 4.

Amakali (1995, p.34), explains the role and qualities of the clan leader among Aandonga:

A clan leader who was rich and owned a large herd of cattle and a large number of mahangu storage baskets (iigandhi), was expected to make a significant contribution towards the settlement of the debt. Such a person could rescue many clan members from dying of hunger as he possessed enough food. If he died, then the clan members held a meeting to elect his successor who should possess the following qualities: a miser, economical person, wealthy, wise and diligent. The elected person inherits the clan wealth and must be prepared to come to the aid of the entire clan, should it experience difficulties.

Every clan has its oohekulu yezimo [clan fathers who are its uncles and leaders]. As among Ovaherero, Ovakwanyama and Aandonga, clan fathers had “power of life and death over members of his clan” (Loeb 1962, p. 286). A leading clan father keeps the

clan’s wealth and distributes the property of the deceased in the clan. This clan leader is called Onkungulu. The criteria for his promotion are owino (experience), common sense, trust, uungundja [expertise in managing wealth]. He can assign someone he trusts to do the distribution on his behalf. Niikondo (2018) explains that onkungulu yezimo is the pillar of the clan whose role is to unite it. Niikondo further claims old people in their 60s and 70s are heads of the clans and are highly respected.

Like a king, a clan father was fortified to enable him to carry out his functions properly. Maria, a daughter of a well-known medicine man, Hamutoko, said that a clan father oha tumbikwa [is made stronger mentally and physically]. Both Maria and Nditya confirmed etumbiko [the empowering] but could not remember the rite or the medicine used for this purpose. The noun okutumbikwa means to prepare someone, especially a young person, to be stronger mentally and physically. The claws of either a vulture or an eagle were put on sinews around the neck of a young boy or a puppy (Nditya and Hamutoko, as cited in Ndamanomhata, 2001). The idea behind this was to make the boy or a puppy eagle-eyed; it was the wish that the positive char acteristics of seeing very far be transferred either to a boy or a puppy. The word is also used when, for instance, young persons are trained to carry out tasks that are beyond their natural possibilities (Ndamanomhata, 2001).

Ndamanomhata (2001) further describes a clan father as follows:

The system of clan fathers as leaders of their clans kept the next of kin together. This had remarkable advantages in traditional Owambo society: the children of sis ters and half-sisters were able to know, and to relate to each other; they were able to be taught which taboos were associated with their clan and which were not. For instance, youngsters were advised from which clan and household they could marry and from which not. Obedience, good manners, hard work, a good upbringing, wealth, status and physical appearance were some of the criteria for choosing a life partner. The sisters and half-sisters of the clan fathers had the significant task of educating their juniors to know their clan members, especially their uncles (p.111).

A clan father is also regarded as shimutsikeni (the one who is burdened with difficulties) or ongudhi yezimo (pillar of the clan). In this case the clan leader is called shimutsikeni because he/she is an arbitrator whose one of his tasks is to settle the conflict within the family. He is expected to carry the difficulties of the entire clan. The word ongudhi yezimo is also used for a similar purpose. Haapanen (1958) has collected a proverb that shows that the clan leader is the pillar of the clan: Omutsa a kwata uuyinga waa tengenye=

Omukuluntu oha koleke ezimo (The poor one holds the small horns intact= The elder makes a clan strong and united).

The role of tategona and memegona

Generally, among the various Aawambo totemic clans, tategona and memegona perform different roles. These roles are performed throughout one’s entire life, from birth to death. Child-rearing is not considered as merely the responsibility of the biological parents, but it extends to the patrilineal and matrilineal relatives of both the mother and the father. The patri lineal and matrilineal responsibilities towards the child are based on the culturally prescribed gender roles.

#### Tate[gona]

Tirronen (1986, p.398) defines “hegona as my /our father’s brother or sister.” This definition requires expansion because uuhegona (the state of being one’s father maternal relative is associated with the task of caring for the son or daughter of one’s male maternal relative).Uuhegona in Oshiwambo is not confined to brothers or sisters but it also applies to the maternal relatives of one’s father, but it is only married women with special qualities who qualify for uuhegona.

Tategona is usually a female relative of the father whose task is to care for a son or daughter of her brother/maternal relative from birth to death. In very rare cases a man may be approached to carry out this task as a tategona, but this is a violation of the traditional norms which regard a woman as a source of fortune (Oye ha tema omulilo gwelago).

Any father selects a hegona or shenkadhi for his child. Such a woman is given the responsibil ity to take care of the child until the child dies- if hegona dies, a maternal relative of hegona is appointed. For one to become a hegona of someone’s child, certain requirements should be met: a) She should be legally married; b) she should have reached menopause; c) she should have a stable marriage; d) she should have a proven record of exemplary behaviour; and e), in today’s context, she must be financially stable. A woman who has abandoned her husband (i.e. divorced or separated) or who mistreats her husband would not qualify to become hegona. It is believed that the abuser of men or the divorcee would contaminate someone’s daughter or son with such bad qualities.

Aawambo believe that an unmarried woman does not have the authority to preside over marriage because she has not gone through the same procedure. Oshiwambo marriage is legalised through the official handing over of omasiga (cooking stands) to the married

woman by her mother-in-law or by hegona. A woman who has not officially been given the omasiga is not considered legally married, according to the Oshiwambo custom. Similarly, a man who has not eaten the sorghum or bran porridge, inaa lya oshithima shiilyawala nenge shuuhutu, is not legally married. Among Ovakwanyama, marriage is also legalised through the offering of fresh mopane sticks to a woman, okundwangulilwa oifonono. It is thus logical that hegona should have been in a stable marriage over a long period, having exercised patience and has gone through the thick and thin of marriage. This is necessary because hegona is considered a chief advisor to the married couple. The parents of the bride and groom believe that hegona would transfer her experience in marriage to the newlyweds. Due to the modern financial demands of weddings, some parents also take into account the financial position of hegona in the hope that she will be able to sponsor the wedding (i.e. with cash, wedding materials or a cow).

The roles of tategona are very specific. At the birth of a child, the mother and the baby spend a few days in seclusion. After the umbilical cord has fallen off, the mother and the baby are released from seclusion by hegona of the child. Hegona introduces the baby to its future gender roles i.e. the baby boy is introduced to cattle herding and the baby girl is introduced to either the cooking or pounding area. The official name of the baby born to an unmarried father is bestowed upon a baby by its hegona. Similarly, the first born baby (osheeli) of a married man is given a name by its hegona who often is its paternal grandmother. This explains why it is common, among the Aawambo, that most of the sheeli are named after a relative of the father. On the occasion the baby receives an official name, it receives a traditional necklace (ondigu), a baby carrier (ondhikwa) and a belt, for a boy, or omuhanga (traditional beads) for a girl from the paternal clan. Ondigu is very significant, in that it is passed on from one child to the next (from osheeli to ontowele and so on) in a married family. However, the necklace of the twins is not passed on, because twins are special babies whose mother undergoes cleansing. Children born out of wedlock or before marriages are treated differently (i.e. each receives a different ondigu). Ondhikwa is considered sacrosanct because a mother may never leave it behind when she has a baby. Besides, ondhikwa symbolizes the generational existence of a clan, hence the use of phrases, such as ngame ogwondhikwa yangandi [I hail from so and so’s ondhikwa], ke shi gwondhikwa yetu [he or she does not hail from our ondhikwa]. This means that the word ondhikwa can also be used to refer to a lineage.

At baptism, in several ethnic groups of Aawambo, the godparents are also the hegona of the child. According to the Code of Canon Law, “Insofar as possible, one to be baptized is to be given a sponsor who is to assist an adult in Christian initiation, or, together with the parents, to present an infant at the baptism, and who will help the baptized to lead a Christian life in harmony with baptism, and to fulfill faithfully the obligations connected

with it” (No. 872). In other words, godparents share the child-rearing and disciplining with the biological parents. A godparent is also expected to present the child with gifts on a baptismal day, to strengthen the ties between himself and the child’s parents. This is similar to the traditional naming ceremony where the child is presented with traditional gifts by hegona. This justifies the trend among several Aawambo ethnic groups to choose a hegona as a godparent.

This responsibility of hegona continues at the confirmation of a child. The hegona is expected to accompany his godchild to church on the day of confirmation. In the church, he is required to accompany his godchild to the altar, to take Eucharist with his godchild. Besides, hegona usually presents his child with a Bible and a hymn book.

Hegona is expected to perform various activities during the wedding ceremony. On the day the banns are made in the church, hegona moves to the homestead of the groom or bride and lives there until the wedding period is over. During her stay she cares for the bride or groom, for example, she cooks for her daily. She also conducts other activities such as moulding cooking stands, lighting the wedding fire and so on. She communicates with ancestral spirits before conducting any activity. However, due to Christianity, some oohegona avoids communicating with the ancestral spirits. It is important to present the important activities carried out by hegona during the wedding period to give a clear picture of the procedure followed:

First, she smears the bride or groom to be with red ochre mixed with fat. The mixture is applied to the whole body of the bride or groom to be. The bride or groom usually remains dressed in shorts while the hegona does her job. This takes place at the oshoto shondjugo (the reception area opposite the mother’s hut) of hegona. People who have accompanied the bride or groom to be are also slightly smeared with the mixture. After performing this activity, the hegona gives the bride or groom a special gift which could be a goat, chicken or money as present. This is to wish the bride or groom luck to receive more gifts for the wedding. Ovakwanyama observe the same practice. However, the bride or groom is to be blessed by hegona in his or her parents’ homestead. This is usually done at the main olupale (main assembly area) or okashotona (the small assembly area opposite the wife’s hut). In some ethnic groups, Aangandjera and Aakwaluudhi, the bride or the bridegroom receives ondjaya from hegona which he/she wears on the right side whereas the one she receives from the memegona is worn on the left side.

The next day, hegona travels to the house of the bride or groom to prepare omasiga (cooking stands) to be used for the wedding party. Accompanied by several women, in the morning, she collects clay from an anthill and moulds it into cooking stands. Afterward, other women follow suit. The moulding of cooking stands usually takes place in the husband’s section in the homestead. Thereafter, hegona erects a mopane tree in the homestead to wish the bride or groom good luck.

After making the omasiga, she makes the main fire. Everybody who wants to make a fire during the wedding ceremony takes a coal from the main fire to light a new fire. When she makes omasiga or fire, she performs various rituals, such as putting a needle under the fireplace. If for any reason hegona is delayed, on the day of singing, referred to as omaimbilo or eeshungi or eemhoko (the day before the wedding day) and arrives late, the house dwellers may decide to make fire. But as soon as they see her coming, such fire is extinguished so that she makes a new fire. It is believed that the fire made by a person other than hegona is not blessed. Thus, hegona may be referred to as gwombambi (duiker/ buck) or gwosizi (tortoise) since these animals symbolise good luck in Oshiwambo. It has to be noted that these designants may apply to both men and women.

A day before the ceremony hegona brews beer, ta hanga omalovu. She boils the sorghum flour and makes the first beer. Nobody else is allowed to make beer before she is done. She also performs various magics as she makes beer, for example, she stirs the beer with a sorghum stalk and puts beer in her mouth and blows it into the air to wish the wedding good luck and also to give the omalovu to the ancestors to taste.

On the wedding day before the procession enters the homestead, the bride and bridegroom are seated somewhere outside the homestead. Hegona of the bride or bridegroom, depending on where the procession is, moves closer to couple to give the bride and bridegroom the omalovu (beer), she takes a cup and takes the beer out of the pot and pours into the cup until it overflows. The beer that spills is given to the ancestors to taste first. She does it carefully so that the beer does not bubble. If it bubbles, then it is believed that one is swearing at the ancestors who in turn curse the newlywed couple. She gives a cup first to the bridegroom and then to the bride. Both the bride and bridegroom receive the cup with both hands and empty the beer without pausing. As she pours beer into the oshitenga, she calls out the name of the ancestors. For example, at one wedding ceremony in 2017, hegona uttered these words:

Niilwa yAamutenya ileni tu fukalakeni

Marta gaShilongo ileni tu lyeni ohango yokaana ketu. Angula dhaSheya penduka tu tante ohango yokaana ketu

(Niilwa daughter of Sheya come and join us in leading our child to adulthood. Marta daughter of Shilongo, join us in enjoying the food for our child’s wedding. Angula, the son of Sheya wake up and join us in dancing for the celebration of our child’s wedding).

Afterward, hegona takes the baskets and puts in the gifts. Before she does this, she waves efungu (dancing wand) and ululates. Hegona is given special meat at a wedding such as front leg in some ethnic groups, or omapitakati (rib cage) in Ondonga from each ox slaughtered. In some Aawambo ethnic groups for example among the Aakwaluudhi, hegona is given the omugongo (the back part of a slaughtered ox). If hegona does not receive sufficient meat, she becomes angry and leaves the party. If that happens, then she curses the couple to become childless. To appease the anger of hegona the couple travels to the homestead of hegona to tender an apology and to ask her to remove bad luck. They shower her with lots of gifts and help her with house chores for a couple of days. When she is satisfied, she performs magic to purify the couple. Once the couple is cleansed of back luck, hegona will massage the tummy of the wife and say: Ka vale nokomushila (Go and produce more children).

When the couple decides to establish their new homestead, they inform hegona of the husband. Hegona will do the mapping of the new homestead. She gets up at dawn and leads her/his child to the new dwelling. Hegona leads the procession and carries the burning omusindilo (a magic stick). When the procession arrives in the new dwelling, the people put their luggage at olupale where hegona makes the fire to officially inaugurate the new dwelling. The owner of the house and his entourage warm themselves before the fire. After a certain period, hegona comes back to bless the entrance. Again, she performs his/her magic.

As mentioned, hegona plays an important role in the life of his or her godson or daughter, thus it is important to illustrate this role with a specific example of hegona of omukwaniilwa (the king), as found in Hahn, Vedder & Fourie (1928, p.110) This example describes the inauguration of a new homestead and the entrance:

The hegona takes a leading part both in the erection of the kraal and in the accom panying ceremonies. Custom demands that when he arrives at the Chief’s kraal he must find the side the main entrance closed and a temporary entrance made on the side facing the new site. To leave an old home for good through its main entrance is oshidhila (taboo). On arriving at the closed up main entrance, the hegona calls upon the chief’s servants to bring fire from the Chief’s fireplace. With this fire, he kindles a large fire just outside the newly made temporary entrance, close to which are

also placed two pots of beer and some baskets of porridge (oshifima). Hegona then seats himself near the fire and calls upon the people waiting outside to come and share a meal. The food is served by the first servant of the senior wife and is quickly devoured. The uncle now enters the kraal through the temporary gateway and proceeds to the part of the kraal, called oshinyanga, in which the tribal fire is kept burning, taking with him an empty wooden milk bucket (etenga) which he hands to the guardians of the fire, known as aatemi yomulilo. The two guardians fill the etenga with cold water to which the hegona adds the sediment of beer and the crushed green leaves of the eshila bush. This mixture is called etalaleko and with it, he proceeds to find the Chief. The two then make their way through the temporary entrance to the site of the new kraal. The hegona there selects the exact spot which will be occupied by the Chief’s cattle within the new kraal and, after having marked out its area, known as oshitemagunda, with a stick, sprinkles the etalaleko over it.

The hegona now proceeds to lay out the plan of the remainder of the kraal. Furrows are quickly drawn and deepened to hold the poles and stakes provided by the ethnic group, and such material can be used from the old kraal.

About three weeks later instructions are issued by the chief to hegona to make arrangements for the inauguration of the new kraal. The latter arrives shortly after accompanied by his wife and women servants’ meal. Six men, each carrying a stout and selected pole to build the main entrance, known as eelo, and a branch of thorn bush, accompany him also. On reaching the homestead hegona is taken in to see the Chief. After the usual greetings, he presents the beer to the Chief, who at once partakes of it with the donor. The chief then gives hegona a pot of his own beer and a small basket containing corn husks. Hegona then leaves the homestead, plac es the beer near the spot where the main entrance is to be erected and sprinkles the husks on the actual site.

In addition to the inauguration of the homestead, when her daughter or son passes on, the hegona is officially informed, because no fire is made before she comes to make the holy fire for the mourning period. If she is delayed, and someone makes the mourning fire before she arrives, then such fire is extinguished as soon as she arrives. She makes the first fire for the mourning session. Anybody who wants to make new fire takes a coal

from the main fire. At the end of the mourning period, hegona is expected to remove the ashes from the main fireplace and throws it in the omudhime bush or simply on the main road. It is believed that the ash thrown in the omudhime symbolizes that death is reduced within the family. When people walk over the ash from the mourning period, they become contaminated with death, i.e. death will strike their families. It is also the responsibility of hegona to do the mapping of the grave of their daughter or sons.

In times of death, Shigwedha and Nampala (2006) maintain that the fire was set by a highly trusted individual who knew the family secret, and who was of the paternal clan of the deceased. For instance, if the deceased was from the Omukwanangombe (the cow) clan, then such responsibilities would be given to a certain elderly man or woman from that clan, though he or she may not have been related to the deceased but were of the same clan.

Hegona does not simply bless homesteads and children. He/she also had the task of blessing the two puppies. If a dog of someone gives birth to two puppies at the same time, the puppies are taken to the homestead of one’s hegona for blessing. Before the owner of the puppies proceeds to the homestead of hegona, beer is brewed which he/she takes along to the homestead of hegona. The owner of the bitch takes two puppies and two calabashes of beer to the homestead of hegona. The two calabashes of beer represent the two puppies. The puppies are handed over to hegona for blessing. Hegona then ululates as she receives the puppies while praising them:

Iyaloo eehamba dange

Iyaloo eehamba dange (Thank you, my twins Thank you, my twins)

Hegona then takes the fat mixed with red ochre and rub the puppies. The bitch itself is also smeared with the mixture. Hegona also prepares beer for the guests and two baskets of porridges. The puppies are left in the care of the hegona. This custom is on the wane among the Aawambo, but there are still a few Aawambo who practise it.

It must be pointed out that hegona also blesses the twin calves in similar fashion. When a cow gives births to two claves, the owner of the cow makes arrangement to take calves to the homestead of the hegona for blessing or alternatively hegona visits the homestead to conduct the ritual in honour of the two calves in oshigunda ( cattle kraal).

There is a threat to the concept, hegona nowadays. The meaning of the concept, hegona has been distorted by several westernised people at present. The impact of Christianity and its western didactic import seems to have negatively influenced some deoshiwambonised people to attach wrong meanings to the concept. A few Aawambo today appoint men as the hegona of their daughters or sons during their wedding. This is wrong because

aalumentu ihaa kwiinine. Some deoshimbonised people appoint their daughters or sons to become hegona of their broth ers or sisters. It has also become a common practice to appoint unmarried women or men as hegona. This is totally in conflict with the traditional practice of Aawambo. Worst of it some men appoint their wives as hegona. Some men also appoint themselves as hegona of their sons or daughters. The biological father or mother cannot become hegona. In Oshiwambo a father may be referred to as he, not hegona. Once a father calls himself a hegona, it means he has relinquished his responsibility as the biological father, because he has regarded himself as the father in a small way. There are also several occasions on which the husband appoints their wives as hegona to conduct ritual for the bride or groom. Again, this is the worst mistake, because the wife takes the red ochre mixed with butter from the ohanga yezimo lyawo (a container in which fat mixed with red ochre is kept belonging to her clan). The ohanga is kept by hegona and the omugwayo (ointment) from ohanga is not applied to aayeni (non-clan members). If hegona dies, then it is inherited by her eldest daughter who then acts as a hegona for the man or woman for whom her deceased mother acted as a hegona. In other words, ohanga is transferred from other to her daughter. It would be appropriate to practice uuhegona consistently in accordance with our tradition. However, some Christians claim: Tse Aakriste katu na shidhila/shipwe (We Christians do not stick to taboos). This is said when they want to appoint unqualified people for the role of hegona.

The role of hegona is, however, still significant in the modern world. When one has bought a car, he/she takes it to the homestead of hegona for blessing. The owner of the car informs he gona that he will come to show him his/her new car. On the day set, the owner of the car drives to the homestead of hegona. When the owner of the car arrives, who usually brings along a calabash of beer, he/she moves in the homestead and exchanges greetings with hegona.

Afterward, hegona takes her olutengwa (a container containing fat mixed with ochre), moves with the owner of the car toward the place where the car is parked. Hegona then takes a horsetail waving it around as she dances and ululates while moving around the car inspecting it. Hego na then takes olukula and rubs the entire car with it. Once she is done, she ululates again and praises the owner of the car and the paternal clan of the car owner. The owner of the car is not allowed to wash off olukula. Olukula has to remain on his car until it falls off by itself. It is believed that washing off of olukula means washing off your luck and blessing. Some oohegona today only rub the tires of the car with the olukula. This custom is still common among Aawambo because cars are still seen smeared with olukula. This action is repeated for every car that is bought. However, there are some oohegona who shun this practice and regard it as an unchristian practice. Instead of rubbing the car with olukula, they simply shake hands with the owner of the car and

thank him or her. In modern days, some oohegona use vaseline or body lotion instead of

olukula. Nonetheless, the use of vaseline contradicts Oshiwambo culture.

#### 8.0.0.2 Meme[gona] and Tatekulu

In this case, meme includes both male and female relatives of one’s mother of the same generation as one’s biological mother. The roles of meme are different from those of tategona. While tategona is entrusted with active roles in most ceremonies in one’s life, meme is mostly responsible for settling the debt incurred by a clan member.

Meme or memegona takes care of his /her sisters’s children when a sister passes on or when the children of the sister are in need. She/he can raise the children of her/his sister. In modern time meme can foot the medical bill of his /her sister’s children and pay educational fees.

The maternal clan (ezimo lyokoomeme) also plays a role in the life of a child. If someone commits an offense the whole maternal clan is held responsible for such an offense e.g. If a boy impregnates a girl, then the maternal clan has to raise money to pay for the damage. If a boy absconds or dies, the payment still has to be made because all clan members are pronounced guilty by the girl’s clan. The maternal relatives do not refuse to make their contribution towards a payment because they are often afraid of ontiko (contagious magic

A child can inherit property from its mother and maternal relatives. If a clan practices witchcraft, the ability to practice witchcraft or sorcery is passed on to the children by the mother. If the father is the omulodhi (wizard/ witch), the children would not be considered to have the ability to practice witchcraft but when the mother practices witchcraft; all the children would be regarded as aalodhi (witches/ wizards).

Explaining the role of the maternal clan in paying debts incurred by a clan member, Amakali (1995) narrates as follows:

In the ancient time, when one’s clan member killed a person, it was said that the totemic clan of the murderer stored a person in a hut (okwe mu pungula kondunda). The complaining clan thus requests that their clan member be released from the hut (i.e. a fine must be paid as a sign of atonement). People gave cattle as payment for the offense committed by their clan members. The whole clan of the culprit was held responsible for the transgression and was liable to make the payment collectively. It was said ihadhi olele kezimo, debts do not remain permanently within a clan. Clan leaders held a meeting and resolved to settle the debts(p. 34).

Closely allied to memegona is the tatekulu (mother’s brother). This tatekulu is is also addressed as meme. The role of tatekulu is not as broad as that of tatekulu. Miettinen (2005) argues that a social father is under a rigorous regime of matrilineality, i.e. a maternal uncle was not without importance in the life of the individual among Aawambo. He was a highly respected person, but his role was activated only when something unusual happened. The special respect for a maternal uncle is seen, for example, in the fact that while the father was called tate, maternal uncle was called either tatekulu or kuku, terms indicating that the person addressed is more senior and more respected than mere tate. A person’s maternal uncle was regarded as his or her “best friend,” and a boy was expected to take constant small gifts to his tatekulu.

The uncle’s role is activated if there is a major conflict between the father and his son or daughter, for example. If things go badly, a person could leave his or her father’s homestead and move to live with this uncle, because he was regarded as the protector of his nephews and nieces and was thus obliged to give them shelter in time of need. Shelter is also given when the children’s mother dies, for in such a case the children do not normally remain with their father but instead the guardianship passes to maternal relatives. An uncle also has the right to be involved in cases his nephew committed serious wrongdoings and is expected to punish him, even to kill him if necessary. This is something a biological father is not allowed to do.

However, tatekulu has little influence on the life of his sister’s children in the modern- day, because there is a propensity to promote the nuclear family system. The Maintenance Act (No. 9 of 2003) makes it obligatory for the biological father to support his offspring. Besides, Westernisation and its didactic import have resulted in people abandoning their extended family system, to a certain extent.

In light of the roles of both hegona and memegona, it seems obvious that children belong to the paternal-cum-maternal clan. Nowadays, children can inherit from their fathers. Children can also succeed their father as headmen. Modern laws that make it obligatory for the children to inherit from their fathers have, to a certain extent, forced Aawambo to abandon some of the old practices though not entirely.

The role of epulangunga

Epulangunga is a fearless person within the clan whose task is primarily to demand for the payments of debts especially in case the debtor hesitates to settle his/her debt. It is praised as:

Enima le ku tumwa li velifa Oudu woye nau kalelele

Vakweni nava ufanwe Noshikefa landeni Ongobe yeefya nai uye

(This rascal if sent to collect you

You must pretend to be ill Your illness must be chronic Let your relatives be invited

Let the coffin be bought

Let the ox for mourning period be bought)

This verse refers to the aggressive attitude of epulangunga. It is a merciless character who can easily resort to killing a debtor. Epulangunga threatens the debtor with physical attack and death. The words coffin and mourning ox are mentioned to show that epulangunga resorts to violence which leads to the death of the victim.

Every clan has epulangunga (male person whose main role in the clan or lineage is to demand in name of his relative, the payment of a debt from somebody). Such a male is fearless, merciless and irascible. He is approached by any clan member to confront the debtor. He is praised as Epulangunga, enima lyowala (The one who demands debts, a worthless person). Enima lyowala in this context refers to the state of being inconsiderate because epulangunga is expected to exhibit rough and irrational behaviours when he demands payment of debts. Besides, clansmen appear to have little respect for him, but they rely on him when a debtor shows reluctance.

Furthermore, epulangunga threatens the clan of the debtor with severe punishment if the clan of the debtor fails to pay the debts within the prescribed time. The epulangunga is often armed when he goes to demand the debts from a certain lineage or clan. He can either be accompanied by a clansman or confront the clan of the debtor alone. The merit of uupulangunga is that it often results in the immediate settlement of the debts, but the demerit of this function is that the epulangunga can attack the debtor or any of the debtor’s clansmen, thereby dragging the clan of epulangunga into legal battle. An informant remarked that: “Otali vulu okweeta oshikolo kezimo. Otali vulu oku kwe

etela ashike olya pumbiwa puudhigu nopokuma tapu nyengathana. Otali ku mangulula” (Epulangunga may plunge the clan into trouble. He can land you into trouble but he is needed when difficulties become unbearable and when the im possible comes one’s way. He frees one from trouble).

#### 5.3.12 Clan system and administration of justice

A clan leader advises members of a totemic clan to tell their children to refrain from committing a serious offense that may bring the name of the entire clan into disrepute or which may cause the clan to be fined for the wrongdoing. According to Oshiwambo tradition when a member of the clan commits an offense, all members of that clan are held responsible for the payment of an offense. The guilty person undertakes a trip to see members of his or her clan, telling them that Onde eta oshikolo kezimo (lit. I have brought debt to the clan, i.e. I have committed a serious offense which makes the entire clan liable for payment of the offense I committed). But in most cases, a close relative conveys a message to all clansmen.

Jazzie (2010) reports a similar situation among the Navajo of North America:

The Navajo use k’e (term which means a wide range of deeply felt emotions which create solidarity of the individual with his/her or her clan). The Navajo use k’e to achieve restorative justice where there is a dispute the procedure which we call“talking things out” works like this: Every person concerned or affected by the dispute or problem receives a notice of gathering to talk things out. At the gathering, everyone has the opportunity to be heard. In the vertical legal system, the zone of dispute is defined as being only between the people who are directly involved in the problem. On the other hand, as Navajo, if my relative is hurt that concerns me; if my relative hurts another I am responsible to the injured person (p.343).

This shows that considering an individual member of a clan‘s offense as an offense that direct ly affects the entire clan appears to be a universal phenomenon. As is a case among Aawambo, the Navajo of North America believe that if a member of their clan is injured then the entire clan is injured. If a member of one’s clan hurts another person, then the entire clan is held responsible for the offense thus every clan member attempts to attend the peacemaking gathering to contribute the solution of the problem.

Among Aawambo members of the clan do not refuse to contribute to the settlement of payment as they fear that members of another clan may enlist the service of a traditional healer to curse their clan (ontiko). Loeb (1962), writing on the penalties for assault and murder among Ovakwanyama explains:

When a person killed a member of another clan, revenge was necessary whether the victim was a man, a woman, or a child, and whether the killing was deliberate or unintentional. Only members (ovakwawo) of the slain person’s clan, however, might execute such vengeance legally. The murderer’s clan tried to save his life by hiding him. His family also went into hiding. The matter was an end and the quilt erased only when the victim’s clanmates had killed either the murderer or one of the murderer’s clanmates.

In some instance a compromise was possible. A murderer and his relatives hurried to round up cattle to give to the dead man’s clan. The peace party that brought the gifts place marula nuts (iigongoti) on the ends of their spears to show their intention, i.e. to show that they appeal for forgiveness and refrain from retaliation. When eight or ten head of cattle had been presented to the aggrieved clan, members of this clan re moved the murderer’s intestines and stretched them on the ground. Beside them, the guilty clan must lay out an equal length of ostrich shell beads. They must also give a brick of salt of the same size as the dead’ man’s head. The final step, the payment of a ball of tobacco insured peace. A pipe was passed around and each person took a few puffs. Smoking the pipe of peace was called okunwa kombiya.. (p.71).

Although Loeb describes the situation among Ovakwanyama, this situation appears to be a global trend, as Freud (1919) reports:

The social side of totemism is primarily expressed in a sternly observed command ment and a tremendous restriction. The members of a totem clan are brothers and sisters, pledged to help and protect each other; if a member of the clan is slain by a stranger the whole ethnic group of the slayer must answer for the murder and the clan of the slain man shows its solidarity in the demand for expiation for the blood that has been shed.

Similarly, Miettinen (2005), writing on the clan system of Aandonga, claims that:

The matriclans were also something that might be called units of common legal responsibility and action. Native Commissioner C. H. L. Hahn claimed that in civil cases it was the clans, and not individuals, who were plaintiffs. Court orders which nominally were against individuals were in reality against the whole clan, as the clan was liable for all the actions of its members. The means of dealing with murder is highly illustrative in this respect; murder was normally compensated for with a certain number of cattle forfeited to the matrilineal relatives of the victim, and the murderer’s clan was liable to pay this if he was unable to do so. If no compensation was paid, the relatives of the deceased had the right to exact blood vengeance, which could be directed against a close relative of the murderer, at least in cases where the murderer himself was not available for punishment. Vengeance was not allowed, however, if the murderer’s clan had cleansed the guilt by performing a certain sacrificial rite, or if the king had pardoned the murderer (p.111).

Furthermore, Tönjes (1996) makes similar observations regarding the jailed murderer in Uukwanyama:

When a murderer was apprehended, he was chained and placed in stocks. He could only be released upon payment of a large fine, between eight to ten head of cattle. If he was unable to pay his fine, his clanmates usually made the payment for him. As soon as possible, his relatives brought in a large ox as the first installment, to prevent t his sale or execution. Not infrequently, however, the rich relatives of an unpopular criminal simply left him to his fate.

When the criminal himself could not be caught, the aggrieved clan tried to seize another member of his family- his mother, brother, sister, uncle or aunt. Propitiation was sometimes necessary when a killing occurred during a cattle raid by Omukwanyama upon an Omukwanyama kraal owner, even though the raid had been carried out by the king’s orders. If one of the king’s men was killed, the killer’s clan had to pay (p.72).

Tönjes further narrates that if the thief could not be taken away and when he possessed no assets, his relatives were held reprehensible. If a thief has stolen too many items, and his relatives were unable to pay, then, one of his relatives was sold as a slave into slavery. The thief that was caught was tied up until his relatives paid the compensation to have

him redeemed. The payment of the stolen item was determined according to its value “When the thief dies, before the payment for the stolen property was made, the relatives had to bear the responsibility for the payment.” (Tönjes, 1996, p. 110). There is a saying in Oshiwambo: Oongunga ihadhi olele kezimo (lit. the debts do not get rotten in the clan, i.e. clan members must be prepared to settle the debts at all costs).

When the thief was killed while stealing items from a homestead, his family members feel offended and asked questions which demonstrate that they are not happy with what had happened to their relative, they then demand that the clan of the killer pay for the blood spilled. “This payment includes gild, shells, beads, tobacco, and cattle. After the payment of the fine, the peace pipe was shared and the affair was at the end. When the payment was not made promptly, someone in the killer’s clan might be put to death or some of the clan’s cattle might be seized in retaliation.” (Loeb, 1962,p. 86). Gibson, Larson, and McGurk (1981) observe similar situations among the Sambyu of Kavango. It is clear that “death does not clear the murderer‘s guilt, for a guilty person’s heir not only inherits his assets but his guilt as well. The family of a deceased offender is responsible for paying his fines. If a wronged person dies, his matrilineal relatives or his father takes up the case” (p.146).

This means death is not seen as a way of exonerating the clan members from payment of debts owed to another person by the deceased thus after the corpse is buried, a meeting is held so that the debtor may reveal how much or what the deceased owed him/ her. Even in modern times, a question is asked by the chairperson of the meeting, who is usually the headman or his representative. The chairperson asks, “Opu na ngu wa lilwa sha kunakusa nenge wu na sha we mu pele ndee ine shi galula. onawa wu shi popye mpaka montaneho yaakwazimo (Is there anyone who is owed something by the deceased or who gave the deceased something which he did not return. It is good to say it in the presence of the clan members). If there are people who are owed, then they reveal it so that members of the clan can discuss later how to settle the debts. Besides, if any clan member is told by the deceased that so and so is indebted to the deceased, the clan member informs the gathering that so and so owed the deceased something. The respondent then rises to either defend himself or admits that quilt and explains how he would settle his debts. The respondent cannot appeal for the write off of the debts, because he/she is aware that the relatives of the deceased demand the payment from the clan not necessarily from an individual.

It seems the mutual responsibility of clan members is also a common trend in the first world as Goodwin (2009), writing on American Indian culture, explains:

The clan plays an important role in cases of murder, rape, and injury whether accidental or intentional. Unless settlement was reached through payment, a blood feud resulted between the clans involved. Material payment varied with the degree of harm and the social position of the injured person. Settlement was attempted by tactful members of the offender’s clan, not necessarily blood relatives, accompanying the emissary. After satisfactory payment was made, speakers emphasised that no further bad feeling should exist between clans. The injured individual or close blood kin in his clan might in some cases seek revenge by destroying the property of the offender or even of a member of the latter’s clan if closely related by blood. In atonement payments, those concerned usually were closely related to the defendant by blood as well as clan affiliation (p.402).

This suggests that clansmen would protect and assist one another in times of need. It appears that the relatives of the offender are obliged to pay to avoid being attacked by the clansmen of the complaining clan, thus a clansman who commits an offense for which payment is obligatory approaches his/her clansmen for assistance.

Today among the Aawambo if the family of the offender is not in a good financial standing, his mother may make use of the electronic media to convey a message to the clansmen to assist him/her. A message of this nature was sourced from NBC Oshiwambo radio service:

Ongame[…] ndi li muAshuupa mOndonga. Otandi ithana ezimo lyandje alihe li ye kungame oshoka onda adhika kuudhigu. Ondi na omumwandje a kutha omuntu eyego ohela. Ezimo alihe nali shi uve. Naye endelele akwetu ye ye kungame ote pulwa eyego lyaantu (I am[…] who is in Ashuupa in Ondonga. I am inviting all my clansmen to come to my house because I am in trouble. My son knocked out the tooth of a certain person in our vicinity yesterday. People are demanding that the tooth be paid for. Let them hurry up please and see me as people are demanding their tooth).

This shows that members of a clan do not feel exonerated when a verdict is passed in court. They do not seem to regard the sentence passed in court as the Alfa and Omega in the murder case. In a similar situation, if a man committed adultery with a married woman, he is required to pay onkondelela (adultery fine). If he does not possess cattle, then he is held in seclusion until his relatives pay for the adultery. However, if a man who commits adultery belongs to the same clan as the husband of the wife, the husband does not demand any payment as such demand could weaken the relationship within a clan.

Furthermore, witchcraft was seen as the practice of a particular family. If a clan member is accused of having caused the death of someone through witchcraft, then the whole clan was held responsible. It was believed that omuna gwomulodhi omulodhi (a son or daughter of a witch is also a witch). In this case, an offspring is classified as omulodhi if his/her mother practices witchcraft, but if it was his/her father that was regarded as a witch, and then he/ she was not a witch. The underlying belief is that witchcraft is transferred matrilineally from mother to her child. In Oshiwambo tradition witches are punished severely as discussed by Hiltunen (1986) who claims that the witches were sentenced to death in Owambo. However, this is no longer the case as Aawambo are no longer allowed to take the law into their won hands by the Goivernment and traditional authorities respectively.

Tönjes (1996) describes the severity of witchcraft about clan system:

The penalty for causing death through witchcraft was death. The relatives of the slain person were not allowed to mourn. Such order to execute the witch was given by the king. The relatives would later come to the king and say, “Uncle, you have been angry with your servant. But the fault was his. The king would reply, ‘Yes, I have been angry. He was at fault. These words signified that the family no longer shared the criminal’s guilt (p.70).

According to the Oshiwambo tradition, a criminal may be handed over to the king to be executed. He/she may also be blacklisted by his/her clan. They regard him/her as shinkilishipanda (an evildoer or black sheep). Such an attitude towards an offender is revealed in the following proverb: Nando wa toola oongombe molundago, wa tondwa kezimo lyoye.

= Omusimbakadhona ita nyanyukilwa kaakwanezimo, nando na futwe oongombe odhindji. (Clansmen are never happy with a pregnant girl, even if she has received many cattle as compensation for damage). Furthermore, if a pregnant girl had an abortion, her entire clan was held responsible for the payment of the fine known as ofuto yonkwele (fine for abortion). Different clans in ancient times harboured fear for ofuto yonkwele, thus girls and other women were warned to refrain from abortion because committing such an offense meant a clan had to give many cattle as compensation.

The pregnancy of an unmarried woman is considered a shame to the entire clan. A pregnant girl may be regarded as an outcast by her clansmen. Traditionally, any girl that fell pregnant before marriage was burnt to death or subjected to cruel abortion. This situation forced some girls to flee their polity to seek asylum in other oshilongo.

#### 5. 3. 17The ovalodi vetu (our witches) concept and Aawambo clans

The concept ovalodi vetu in Oshiwambo is not associated with witchcraft. It is used in euphemistic sense to refer to mutual assistance, solidarity or cooperation. In Anthropology this is called joking relationship.

This shows that this concept is used among Africans to minimise conflicts and war between ethnic groups, clans, and lineages and to promote unity in diversity. According to N. Mlambo (personal communication, March 29, 2017), joking relationship among the Shona of Zimbabwe is common in bars, restaurants, workplaces and so on.

In Oshiwambo, close cooperation does not only exist between members of the same clan, but it may also exist between members of different clans. Such clans do not necessarily belong to one phratry but they form alliances. This alliance is inherent in Oshiwambo tradition, for example, traditionally, the hyena is known for attacking and killing cattle, thus members of the Cattle clan see the hyenas as their “enemies”. Members of the cattle clan often jokingly remark: “Mbono ohaye tu li po, onkee oyo aalodhi yetu.” “Those ones eat [bewitch] us up, therefore they are our witches.”

The ovalodi vetu clans say to other clans: aapika yetu (our slaves). Again, the word aapika here is used to mean our victims. These phrases are used in the address form. A member of Aakwanekamba clan says to a member of the Cattle clan: Yapika yetu, mu uhala po (Good afternoon, our slaves). A member of the Cattle clan replies Eeno tu uhala po, yalodhi yetu (Yes, good afternoon, our witches). In Oshindonga, for example, in exchange of greetings between a member of Aakwaambahu clan and a member of Aakwanangombe clan may occur as follows:

Omukwanangombe: Muthingani gwandje? The one who causes me evil?

Omukwambahu: Mulodhi gwandje? My witch?

Omukwanangombe: Wu uhala po? Good afternoon?

Omukwambahu: Eeno otu uhala po inaatu uhala po? Yes, we are fine but we are also not fine.

Omukwanangombe: Oyike? Oyike? What is the matter? What is the matter? Omukwambahu: Omwa lala tatu mu logo? You have been bewitching all night long?

Omukwanangombe: Uuposi weni mbo? That is due to your ignorance.

Omukwambahu: Ano ihamu kuta? Are you never satisfied? Omukwanangombe: Otii ku londeke komulunga. I will put you on a palm tree. Omukwambahu: Aawe, tate nayi kwate inayi lya. No, Sir please forgive me. Omukwanangombe: Otii ku fala mombuga? I will take you into the wilderness.

Omukwambahu: Wuwuuu, awoooo, sila ndje ohenda, tate. Wuwuu, No. Have mercy on me sir (N. Nekondo, personal communication, April 3, 2017).

This is the social jokes cracked between aalodhi and aathingani. As omuthingani apologises she pretends to be trembling with fear while omulodhi makes threats to cause trouble to his/her victims. The children who are not aware of these jokes may cry if they hear the threats against their parents. For example, an informant told us that he was frightened by a member of the Aakwanaamba clan who once said to her: Wu li mpono nyoko a si nalenale. Nyoko otwe mu lya po nale. Twe mu fumbika nokuli (O, you are there while your mother is already dead. We have bewitched her. We have already buried her). In response, the informant, who was a child then, narrated that she cried loudly while running towards her homestead. Upon her arrival at the homestead, she found her mother at home who simply laughed when her daughter told her why she was crying.

Members of clans who refer to one another as witches and blinds establish a clan alliance. This group becomes a cooperative organisation. Members of this group form an alliance and help one another in times of need and in times of joy. They extend a helping hand to one another if there is a wedding ceremony. The “witches” may provide an ox to contribute

towards the wedding ceremony. They brew beers and buy drinks for the ceremony. The “victims” also reciprocate. If death strikes within the “victim’s clan” the witches help with funeral arrangements. Members of this alliance also exchange gifts. If one of them slaughters an ox, he has to ensure that each member receives a large portion. They may organise an intra-group session to discuss current affairs in the community. These people behave as if they belong to the same clan.

Ovalodi are accorded special treatment by aapika. During the wedding, the tail and the buttocks of the slaughtered animal are given to aalodhi to eat. This situation is anchored in the Oshiwambo saying: Onde mu kwata momushila, (I held him /her by the tail, i.e. he is my victim and Onde mu kwata pomatako (I touched his buttocks, i.e I am more powerful than him.). They also pulled down the room of the deceased in the ancient time.

It is interesting to note that the alliance may include five clans or more. This suggests that if authorities make use of such alliances to promote unity in diversity, they are likely to succeed because these alliances transcend tribal or ethnic lines. These alliances may also serve as a bulwark against xenophobia. Kavari (2013) provides Otjiherero proverb which confirms the existence of clan alliance; Eyanda kari kondwa mape he kutja ovandu avehe veyanda rimwe wina omuhoko, mena rokutja ovo va za komuze umwe (Matrilineage is not cut, it is not a stick. This means that people who belong to the same matrilineage are relatives because of the common ancestor. It does not matter whether they know one another).

Since members of an alliance pool their resources together, it is our submission that the alliance may be used to fight against poverty and individualistic tendencies. The African concepts which promote unity such as harambee, ubuntu, uushiinda (neighbourliness) and ujamaa appear to be intertwined with clan alliances. The formation of alliances between different clans refutes Estermman’s claim that “among the Ambo the various clans do not contract alliances with each other.” (1976, p.116).

#### Aawambo clans and taboos

Taboo is an activity that is forbidden or sacred based on religious beliefs or morals. Breaking a taboo is extremely objectionable in society as a whole. Around the world, an act may be a taboo in one culture but not in another. Similarly, a taboo may act in one clan but not in the other clan, there are dos and don’ts in each clan. Aawambo believe that if one transgresses a clan taboo, he or she is likely to land in trouble that may lead to his or her death. Besides, there is a belief that if a taboo is broken by one clan man, the misfortune that befalls the offender becomes contagious, resulting in the death of all clansmen one by one (Frazer, 1910).

Furthermore, it is generally believed that members of a clan may not kill or eat its totem. In some cases, members of the same totem are not allowed even to touch their totem. Our observation among Aawambo is that they eat, kill and touch their totem. When a member of a clan is eating his totem, he may jokingly say: Ngame otandi li omukwetu (I am eating my relative). A member of a different clan may jokingly ask: Oto li omukweni? Are you eating your relative? If a member of one clan finds a member of another clan eating their totem, he may jokingly remark: Oto li po omukwetu. Ohamu li po aantu yomazimo gaantu. Otatu ku geele ngele oto tu li po ngaaka. (You are eating my relative. Do you eat people from other clans? We become angry with you if you eat us like that). But Aawambo do not attack or vent anger on one another for injuring, eating or killing their totems. The taboos on totems have virtually vanished among Aawambo as they eat, kill or injure totems without the fear of being avenged by anyone.

Although Aawambo might have broken the widely accepted observance of totemism, they observe certain clan taboos. Tirronen and Dammann (1975) list some Oshiwambo clan taboos:

The list, however, does not specify the clans which are expected to adhere to the taboos mentioned. Furthermore, it is difficult for the present Aawambo to adhere to these taboos because they are not accompanied by explanations or reasons. Many Aawambo are now eager to interrogate cultural practices. Young Aawambo are very inquisitive and often pose questions on what happens when a taboo is broken. Some of the taboos are no longer in force, for example, the last taboo on the list. Men no longer, strictly speaking, use the name of the clan as the yardstick for selecting their future wives, but they simply consider the good qualities of a woman, regardless of the clan she descends from. Besides,

Aawambo no longer refrain from eating the beef of a pale brown head of cattle. There are, however, certain taboos which certain clans still observe.

Estermann (1976) mentions a few taboos of a few specific clans:

Members of the Ox clan are prohibited from having relations with their wives after they begin to eat mush from new crops before mush offering ceremony (oshipe).The people of the Zebra clan are forbidden to use grain for cooking before having performed the aforementioned rite. The same clan cannot eat the flesh of the reedbuck. Nor are they permitted to wear a belt made from the hide of a yellow ox. For a future mother of the locust people, it is a taboo to give birth outside eumbo of the child’s father, even if the child is illegitimate. In the Snake Clan, a woman must not be married while she is pregnant, even to the man responsible for her pregnancy. Usually, also it is the custom in this clan for a woman to give birth to her child at a kraal[sic] other than that of the child’s father, but in the Grasshopper Clan, it is forbidden for a woman to give birth in any kraal[sic] other than that of her child’s father, even if the child is illegitimate (p.110).

This shows that there are clan taboos that every member of the clan should adhere to. There are taboos among Aawambo which serve as a warning to clansmen. One of them is Ezimo lyetu ihali li omayuku (Our clan does not consume magical concoction to get rich). This saying means clansmen of a particular clan do not turn to magic to become rich. Any attempt to seek the services of a magician to get rich is opposed by all clansmen and whoever breaks this taboo can become mentally deranged. Another taboo is: Ezimo lyetu ihali kutha iinima yaantu (Our clan does not steal). In such a clan, any clan member who attempts to steal may become mentally deranged. Although there is no testimony as to the effectiveness of these two taboos, they serve as a deterrent to misdemeanors.
