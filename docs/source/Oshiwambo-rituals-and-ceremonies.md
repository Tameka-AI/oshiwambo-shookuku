# Oshiwambo rituals and ceremonies

Oshiwambo ritual and ceremonies

1.Initiation ceremony

Before marriage, the girls undergo the ceremony known as efundula/ olufuko. The grown up girls enrol for efundula at Namunganga‘ s house. They are accompanied by their mothers.  The efundula lasts for four days. The first day is called okaombexuxwa. This is the day of preparation. They have to have their hair plaited in a hairstyle known as elende The girls are dressed and decorated.They are given instructions of what is expected of them inside Namunganga ‘s hut. The oingobele (shelter made of mopane branches in which the girls stay) are made.They are put in sun at the oshoto to undergo a pregnancy test. The one who tests positive [when she collapses due to pregnancy]  is disqualified for the ceremony. They have to walk over a stick and passes through the legs of the omupitifi (wedding leader).They are led by mwene woshila (the owner of big herd). The omupitifi beats the drum each a girl passes through. The pregnant girls do not walk over stick. A coal is thrown at a pregnant girl which symbolises the forgiveness of sins and the parents of the pregnant girls are fined for contaminating the ceremony with the filthy girls.

The second day is called okambadyona (little jackal). Dancing continues on this day. The parents make beer and marula oil. Each novice receives a mug of beer from an elderly lady (omupitifi), but before the beer is drunk, the man spills it over the girl who crawls through legs. On this day the girls do the rhythmic movement with head only for about an hour without stopping.

The third day is called ombadje ya kula (big jackal). The oxen are slaughtered. The attendants enjoy foods and drinks. The girls go out of the homestead to the place near the entrance (oluvanda) to meet their bridegrooms. On this day the singing is done by boys before  the sunset..The omhoko (singing- cum- dancing   session) is led boys and girls who sing and dance. Two oongoleka (bestmen/bridesmaids ) are present in eemhoko. A girl holds two dancing wands (omafungu). The bridegrooms and his best friends dance and hit the handhoes against each other. On this day, each girl wears oilenga (strands of fat) to show that an ox was slaughtered for them. Every engaged bride wears a strip of sinew or palm leaf on her wrist. The men proposed the unengaged girls by tying a leaf on their wrists. If a girls cut off the leaf or strip of sinew, it means she has declined the offer.

The fourth day is known as omuuhalo  (day of love). The girls are anointed with the olukula (red ochre). Each bridegroom chooses his best friend (ongoleka) to carry the bride to a treewhere symbolic engagements is made. A girl walks across the forked stick to prove the she is not pregnant. This occasion symbolises the end of the ceremony.

After the fourth day, comes the period of social freedom. During this period the girls are known as oihanangolo or aamati (boys). They are armed with sticks and roam the country. They rub their bodies with ash. They enjoy immunity because when they attack someone she/he should not retaliate. They are permitted to do what they please. They can go in a field and pick corn and beans. They are accompanied by young girls called oufundifi (those who make one skillful, i.e. flower girls). The oufundifi carry baskets filled with ash to enable the oihanangolo to renew the ash on their skins. The oufundifi only rub their faces with ash. The oihanangolo spends about a month in the bush. They wear  omhatela ( four pointed headdress of married woman with a metal attached to it ), a bundle of aloe sticks around their neck, on wrists and ankles. They also wear okateta( apron) at the front attached to the belt and endodi (hairstyle) at the back.The oihanangolo sing as they roam the country . They visit houses to get food.

After they have graduated from this period, they are washed and rubbed with the red ochre. They wear ondjeva  ( ornaments from ostrich eggshells) around waist.  A feast is held in honor of the graduates. People eat oshifima and oondhingu (dry beef cut into rolls). The graduates move to the house together with their mother on this day. The next day the engaged girls are taken to the house of their husband. The bride is collected by his bride who is accompanied by the bridesmaid and bestman. The bridesmaid carries the basket with 8  handhoes in and an arrow which is given to the father of the bride. They hand hoes are given to the mothers of the bride. The best man brings an ox as a bridal price. When the ox is received, the bride and the bridegroom, their bestman and bridesmaid leave for house of the bridegroom. They are given a young girl, who carries the okaaga (a basket in which valuable things such as ointment box, traditional necklaces are kept,) to accompany them. When the bride goes to the house of the bridegroom she walks very slowly and stops for a few minutes from time to time..

2 Modern- cum- traditional marriage

Prior to engagement party, a fiancé goes to the house of his fiancée to introduce himself

He is accompanied by two or three of his friends. There he is interviewed by his in-laws about his background and the name of the clan he belongs to.  The fiancé may then present his girlfriend with the gifts. On the day of engagement, the boy is again accompanied to the house of his girlfriend by his friends.

When the fiancé is ready for marriage he informs his parents who sends man to the house of the fiancée to deliver the message. If the parents agree to the request, both the parents of the fiance and fiancée begin to invite friends, neighbours and relatives. The fiance and fiancée also do the same. Most adults prefer to be invited in person.

On the day the banns is read in the church, before she goes to the church, the oshifonono (fresh mopane stick) is lit and the smoke that comes out  is inhaled by the bride or bride groom. She/he also sits before the fire lit by a paternal relative. She removes the ondjeva (strands of ostrich egg shells worn on girl’s waist) and put on the omushambe (traditional necklace).   After the church service, the fiancé together with his entourage proceed to the house of the fiancée to hoist the two flags. In some cases the fiancé is given the engagement ring on this day.The two flags remained hoisted for a longer period and they are lowered after the wedding on the day to be announced by the married couples.

Two flags are also hoisted in the house of the fiancé. One of the flags is hoisted on the room of the fiancee or fiancé.

The next day, the omafiya (pot stands) are made, this activity may continue for the next two days.

Before the marriage is blessed in the church, the fiancé and fiancée are rubbed with olukula (a mixture of oil and red ochre) by their respective paternal relative to wish them good luck. Such a paternal relative presents a fiancé or fiancée with the first gift. Consequently,fiancé or fiancée goes to the houses of the acquaintances and relatives to collect the gifts such as chickens, goats, money, etc. She is accompanied by the iinkumbi ( bridesmaids and best men) who dances and give shout of praise throughout the journey The collection of gifts usually takes four to five days. Some people deliver the gifts to the house of the fiancé or fiancée by themselves. In fact this the busiest time for the fiancé and fiancée.

Two days before the wedding party, beer is made. A number of women keep themselves with the making of beer.

On the eve of the wedding ceremony which is known as omaimbilo (songs) or eeshungi / eemhoko, a number of people flock to the house of the fiancé and fiancée. Dancing and singing continues throughout the night. The dance is now and then interrupted by eating and beer drinking.

In the morning the fiancé or fiancée prepare herself for the church service. The bride/bride groom gets up at dawn led by paternal relative . The whole procession moves around the house several times until they return to the oshoto shondjugo.It is at this place where they eat and drink before they depart for the church service. The bride/bridegroom is given two women or more to accompany him to the church and also to accompany the bride/brigdegroom to the house of the bride/bride groom.  He/she does not leave the house before the oxen for the house are slaughtered. He/she is accompanied by a number of people to the church. She is given a permission to leave house by a paternal relative.

After the church service the procession proceeds to the house of the bride. The women dance and ululate as they move. Everyone shouts praises in honour of the bride and bridegroom. The praises of the clan of the bride or bride groom is also chanted. When the procession arrives at the bride’s house, it may not move in the house until it is given the green light by the paternal relative of the bride. Each person who has presented the bride with an ox or cow, takes the spear and stabs it in the ground. Another wedding procession [of people who remain in house] come to meet the bride and bride groom. But before the procession enters the house, the bride and bride groom are seated at the table under a shelter or tree. Here a prayer is made. The Paternal relative of the bride moves to the table to give the bride and bridegroom the omalovu (beer), she takes a cup and takes the beer out of pot and pour omalovu into the cup until it overflows. The beer that spills is given to the ancestors to taste first. She does it carefully so that the beer does not bubble. If it bubbles, then it is believed that one is swearing at the ancestors who in turn curse the married couple. She gives a cup first to the bridegroom and then to the bride. Both bride and bridegroom receive the cup with both hands and empty the cup without pausing. She then takes her gifts and put them in the big basket which is on the table. While she does this, she waves efungu (dancing wand) and ululates. The other women ululate as well. If a small child is put in the basket for the gifts, it means the married couple will adopt that child.

After she has officially opened the prize giving ceremony, several people proceed to the table to present their gifts to the bride and bridegroom. Thereafter the procession enters the house where eating and drinking start. On this day the bride and bridegroom eat ekaka (dried spinach). The ekaka is eaten to wish them good luck. The bridegroom is taken to the oshinyanya shondjugo where he is introduced to relatives of his wife. Every person who has given an ox, is given a hind quarter. The paternal relative who is in charge of  the wedding party is given epeta(three ribs with flesh on them)  from each slaugtered ox. The namesake of the bride / bridegroom receives the front leg of each slaughtered ox.

After an hour or so, the procession proceeds to the house of the bride groom where the same procedure is followed.

The bride spends a few days at the house of the bridegroom,a  flower girl  remains behind with the bride while the rest of the people who accompanies the bride departs.  When she goes back to the house of her parents,  she is accompanied by a young girl that is related to the bride. After two or four days she returns to the house of the bride. She brings along her  okaaga (a small basket with the ointment box of a married woman representing the married bond) and some pots to start the kitchen with.

It is important to note that the Aambalantu do not receive an ox or hoes as bridal price, The Aakwaluudhi may be given an ox or  a sheep as bridal price. The Aandonga and Aakwanyama accept an ox only as bridal price.

#### 3.Omitima (heart feast)

Aawambo maintain relationship between parallel cousins. The relationship between parallel cousins is expressed through a heart feast (omitima). This feast is enjoyed by several Aawam bo even today. It is accepted among Christians because it does not involve sacrifices to the ancestors. Loeb (1962) describes how the feast is conducted:

When a man of a certain clan kills an ox, he holds a feast. If he has a male parallel cousin, for example, his father’s brother’s son who belongs to his clan, then he invites his parallel cousin to the feast. This parallel cousin is entitled to the ox’s heart. If the host has no male cousin belonging to his clan, then a female cousin is invited to the feast. Usually, it is a woman rather than a man who claims the heart, presumably because a woman is more able to give a feast in return, and because the women outnumber men. The female cousin arrives on the appointed day, carrying a basket and collect the heart of the slaughtered ox, the lungs and the throat. After enjoying the feast, she takes these parts back to her kraal. Not every female cousin qualifies for omitima; the qualification is based on marital status. It is a married female cousin who qualifies for omitima because she is expected to reciprocate.

When a woman receives an ox heart on two occasions, she is expected to reciprocate.First, she cracks the marula nuts, removes the kernels and makes marula oil (ondjove). The oil is added to the chickens which she prepared for the feast. She also prepares beer. Thereafter she invites her male parallel cousin to her homestead. The male cousin is accompanied by friends. She provides them with mahangu porridge, the beer and the chickens (p.122).

This is a feast between paternal parallel cousins who belong to the same patrilineage. The aim of this feast, as pointed out earlier, is to strengthen ties between “brothers and sisters.” Parallel cousins from either mother or father’s sister regard one another as brothers and sisters. If parallel cousins from the father’s side do not belong to the same maternal clan, they still regard one another as brothers and sisters, though they are not entitled to inherit anything from one another because they have different ancestors in terms of matrilineal line.

#### 4.Omandjandja (Intestine Feast)

Closely allied to Omitima feast, is another feast called Omandjandja (intestines). This feast may also be celebrated in Ongandjera, Uukwaluudhi and Uukolonkadhi. The feast is organised to strengthen ties between paternal parallel cousins. For example, a man from the snake clan slaughters an ox and holds Omandjandja feast. He invites his father’s brother’s daughter, usually a married woman. The invited parallel cousin collects omandjandja on the day set. This woman fills an old basket with mahangu or sorghum and proceeds to the homestead of her paternal parallel cousin. She is accompanied by her friends.

When she arrives, a special receptionist meets her and her entourage. She and her entourage are led to oshinyanga shondjugo (reception area opposite the wife’s sleeping hut). They place their luggage at oshinyanga shondjugo. After the exchange of greetings, she is led to ondunda yonyama (the hut where meat is kept). She takes along her basket. There the basket is emptied and then omandjandja and small pieces of meat are placed in the basket. She dances and ululates (ta tanta ye ta ligola). She then returns to oshinyanga shondjugo and places her basket opposite ondjugo. She then takes off a piece from omandjandja and throws it on the roof of ondjugo while ululating. At oshinyanga, the guests are served beer and food.

The giving of omandjandja to a parallel cousin may also happen during a wedding ceremony, at the homestead of one’s parallel cousin. On this occasion, a similar procedure is followed. However, on this occasion, the recipient of omandjandja expresses her joy:

Iyaloo shili!

Iyaloo shili!

Naku ze ishewe mukweni! Naku ze ishewe mukweni!

Loosely translated:

Thank you!

Thank you!

May you be followed by another sibling! May you be followed by another sibling!

This praise is expressed to wish another sibling to get married so that the recipient of omand jandja may come and get omandjandja again.

The recipient of omandjandja is expected to reciprocate after receiving omandjandja on two occasions, to strengthen the relationship. She can either hold a similar feast or invite her parallel cousin to a wedding ceremony at her homestead and presents her parallel cousin with omandjandja. If invited to a wedding party, she is given a dress and her husband is treated in the same way.

In modern times, when a parallel cousin collects omandjandja, she informs her friends to accompany her. They still take along a basket of mahangu, but may add cases of beer, wine and cool drinks (S. Alweendo, personal communication, December 19, 2016).

#### 5 Omapunga (Lung feast)

There is a ritual held in honour of a parallel cousin whose father has passed on. This ritual is common among Aangandjera, Aakwaluudhi and Aambalantu and is known as omapunga. The word omapunga comes from the verb stem pungauka, meaning to walk in an unsteady way. This means once one’s father is no more, one is no longer strong enough to fend for him or herself. In the Oshiwambo culture, a man is metaphorised as ongudhi (supporting post or pillar). When one’s father passes on, one has no ongudhi to lean on. Hence, the orphans are said to pungauka. A person from the same clan as the father of the orphan invites the orphan to his homestead, usually on a wedding party. For example, a person from Aakwanakatati clan invites an orphan fathered by a deceased man from Aakwanakatati clan.

The invited person fills an old basket with mahangu or millet, or takes along cases of beer, cooldrinks or money, and asks his or her friends to accompany him or her to the homestead of the host. When they arrive at the homestead of the host, they start ululating, taa ligola, as they enter the homestead. They move to oshoto shondjugo, where they put their load. The load is taken to konduda yonyama (The hut in which meat is kept). The basket is loaded with lungs, hearts and a small piece of meat. Later on, the orphan is invited to konduda yonyama to collect t he basket. Upon arrival, he or she is shown the basket and asks one person to help her put it on her head. She then carries the basket to the oshoto shondjugo (assembly point opposite the wife’s hut) and puts it down. She takes a piece from the lungs and throws it on the roof of ondjugo. Throwing a piece of lung on the roof of ondjugo means naku ze ishewe gumwe tu ye tu pewe omapunga (May another

daughter or son get married so that we may be given omapunga). Afterwards, the orphan and her entourage leave while ululating to express their joy. It is said: Na ye nomapunga a ka pungauke nago (lit. May she take omapunga along, so he or she may continue walking in an unsteady way- that is, may he or she find solace in the consumption of these lungs). This custom strengthens ties between the deceased father’s relatives and his children.

Once the parallel cousins are given omapunga on two consecutive occasions, she organises omapunga feast and invites her parallel cousin to her house. She may slaughter an ox or goat. She prepares foods and drinks. On the day set, her parallel cousins, accompanied by her friends and relatives come to the homestead of the host and the feast gets in full swing.

#### 6. Omatunda (Humps feast)

Omatunda is a feast celebrated particularly in Ongandjera. The feast is associated with the wedding party. It is celebrated to cement the relationship between the man whose daughter tied the knot and his paternal priest (shenkadhi).

When there is the wedding ceremony in a certain homestead, the husband collects all the humps from the slaughtered oxen and takes them to the homestead of his paternal priest. Once the paternal priest is given these humps she becomes overjoyed and shrilled to express her joy. In some cases, the humps may be taken to the homestead of the paternal priest during any other joyous occasion other than the wedding ceremony.

After the paternal priest is given humps on two occasions, she prepares a special ceremony called omatunda. She invites her godson to the ceremony. The ‘godson’ in turn invites his neighbours to accompany him to the ceremony. She also invites her neighbours to come to the feast. The neighbours stream to the homestead of the paternal priest. They bring along the gifts such as beers, flours or any other item. The host prepares foods and drinks for the guests. She may slaughter an ox and chickens.

On the day set, the ‘godson’ and his entourage travel to the homestead of the paternal priest. They arrive there in the afternoon and are taken to a special place within the homestead to served with foods and drinks. Women take their baskets to the cooking place. Afterwards, they join the men to enjoy the feast.

7. Slaughter, Skinning, and vision of carcass

The slaughter, skinning, and division of the carcass in Oshiwambo cultures is carried out with great care and in an appropriate (differentiated) manner for celebratory and mournful occasions. Slaughtering an animal cannot simply carried out by anyone. Such a person must be kind and principled, as it is believed that the meat of ox slaughtered by an unkind person becomes tasteless. Within any given community, there are respected people who are known for their slaughtering skills and good character who would therefore be invited to travel to and perform the slaughter at the homestead where the ohango (wedding) or oosa (funeral) is being held. Traditionally, an ox would have been pierced to death with a spear (okutsela, ‘to kill an ox with a spear’, Tirronen, 1986, 433). Indeed, the slaughterman (omutseli) is identifiable by the spear that he carries, which should have cow dung at its tip. This symbolises that an animal is about to be slaughtered and points to the fact that an ox excretes waste matter from its body once it is pierced.

Before he pierces an ox, the slaughterman goes to the left-hand side of an ox and throws a handful of sand at the ox or sits crossed-legged in the vicinity of the ox if it exhibits violent behaviour. It is believed that once this is done, the ox will calm down. Before the ox is pierced, the wife of the slaughterer blows omalovu giilya (mahangu beer) on the spear, blessing the spear so that it the slaughterer may not miss the heart of the animal and that the ox will die instantly after being pierced. However, we note that in contemporary contexts people use a firearm to slaughter the ox. After the ox has fallen, and if slaughtered for a wedding, it is positioned with hind legs facing the east and head facing the west. If slaughtered for the mourning ceremony, a post-slaughter position is not dictated. Whilst some people do consume blood in Owambo, at a wedding this was said to be unlikely due to the abundance of meat available. Therefore, ‘ombinzi tayi zi mo pevi’ (‘the blood goes out onto the ground’, Tate Tomas Nangolo).

The skinning of ongombe yohango (the wedding ox) is led by man who is experienced in such matters. This man has to skin (okuyuya) the ox according to a traditionally acceptable standard to avoid offending the ancestral spirits. The skinner is assisted by young men (aayuyi, ‘skinners’). Tate Tomas Nangolo reported being in a team of around 20 from the village involved in the slaughter, skinning and butchery of the oxen slaughtered for a large wedding in Ondonga, with the team receiving some tripe, intestines, and some liver for their work. The process is as follows: the throat is cut open and then the scrotum is cut off. Thereafter, the carcass is cut from the scrotum to the head, leaving the chest intact because the chest is set aside for the groom and his delegation on a designated date (which date? Or should we just say it is set aside for them?). Next, the skinning of the front legs commences, from the knee to the throat. At the groom’s homestead (is there an ongombe yohango at the groom’s homestead or would it be at the bridal homestead? Or is this for any cow slaughtered at ohango?), the lower parts of the legs are left intact. The right lower part, known as the ekondo, is later cooked un-skinned and given to the omufali gwokondjugo, the female leader of the delegation of the groom. The hind legs are not skinned in the same way (how are these skinned, if not in the same way as this?)

After skinning, the butchery of the wedding ox begins. First, the right hind leg is cut off, then the right front leg. Then, the left front leg is cut off, followed by the left hind leg. The torso is then cut open and the insides are removed, after which the ondete (breastbone) is removed. The skinner takes an axe and carefully cuts off epitakati (three pairs of ox ribs with flesh in one piece). He then cuts off the head and the neck bone. The omapeta (ribs in their entirety) from the right and the left are also carefully cut (the omapeta are comprised of oombati and oluhahani, according to Tate Tomas Nangolo). All the pieces are then taken into the homestead through the okanto, the back entrance. It is believed that if the meat is taken into the homestead through the main entrance, the ehale, it will become tasteless.

During the wedding celebration day, particular pieces of meat are given to specific people. Prior to distribution, the bride or groom’s Hegona enters the ondunda yonyama (meat hut) and inspects all the pieces of meat. This individual might be identified as ‘second father’ and the role is assumed by paternal relative (usually female) at the birth of a child. It is the Hegona who will look out for the child through their life, including overseeing their wedding as the ‘marriage master’, lighting the fire at their wedding and funeral, and mapping out their homestead and grave. Special responsibility for meat distribution is held by omukeeleli gwondunda yonyama (the keeper of the meat hut). Every person who receives his/her piece of meat is expected to give a shout of praise. For example, a man might say ‘uuwuh!’ and a woman might say ‘ilililili!’ to show their appreciation. Silence when receiving the piece of meat indicates that the receiver is displeased with the size or number of pieces they have been given

The Hegona him/herself is given an oshitako shomushila (a hind leg with the tail), and the rumen (stomachs) called ondhikwa and epitakati. The next person to be given his pieces is the mbushe (the namesake) of the groom or bride. The mbushe receives an oshitako shomushila and, from each of the slaughtered oxen, a front leg (oshooko). Each person that has provided an ox is given an oshitako shomushila. In Uukwaluudhi and Ongandjera, a person who has provided an ox is given an oshitako shomushila and olushushwa (part of the backbone) and all the ribs from one side. The etunda (flank) is given to the person who mapped out the homestead of the groom or bride’s father. Certain cuts of meat and offal are special and usually require reciprocal obligations: oshithoneno (the scrotum, given to age-mates to wish them fertility), omapunga (the lungs, given to paternal cousin whose father has passed on), omatunda (the flanks, symbolising strength, and given to a paternal relative who mapped out the homestead of the groom’s father), omutima (the heart, given to a paternal cousin), and omandjandja (the intestines, with the small intestine given to one’s paternal cousin who is married and the large intestine given to a woman who have reached menopause). Once the recipient of any of the above receives a piece twice, whether at a wedding or other occasion, he is obliged to organise a ceremony to thank the one from whom the meat was received. A person may slaughter an ox and invite the person to whom he wants to give these special cuts.

8 Mourning period

When a person passes away, the death is announced by shouting out loudly. The neighbours respond to the shout by running towards the house where the shout comes from. The death of the reigning chief and that of a person struck to death by lightning is announced by ululating, because it is believed that if a person is killed by lightning he/she is killed by God himself. The causes of other death cases are attributed to witchcraft and sorcery. The arriving mourners ask the people already in house: Omu li momutumba? (Are you in the sitting position). The mourners already in the house respond: Ee-ee, ne mwa tondoka/ Ee-ee, ne mwe shi uvu ko? (Yes, did you run?  /Yes, did you hear it?). If the deceased is the husband, the bull, cow and cock are killed, because nobody would be held responsible and pay for damage if these animals injure the animals of the neighbours/ if they hurt other people. The roofs of the iigandhi (mahangu storage baskets) are taken down and the rooftops of the huts are bent slightly to symbolize that the husband is no more. The entrance of the house is sealed with two sticks which cross each other diagonally. It is believed that when death strikes its victim, it passes through the entrance. A new entrance is made so that death fails to find its way in the house again. The corpse is made to sit in the upright position.  The legs are drawn up and folded in front of the chest and the arms are crossed on the chest. The corpse is buried in the upright position to prevent death from spreading to the other people. It is feared that if the corpse is buried while it is lying down more people will die.

If the deceased is a wife the pounding blocks are dug out and the omasiga (three stones or pots on which the pot is placed when cooking) are turned upside down. [The omasiga simbolize the marital bond.] When a woman is no more they are shattered into pieces. Similarly, when a woman annuls marriage, she shatters the omasiga into pieces.

On the first day of mourning, the fire is made by the he-gona (a paternal relative) of the deceased. This fire symbolizes that there is life after death. If the mourners would like to make the fire somewhere else in the house, then they take a burning piece of wood/ coals from the special fire. The mourning period lasts for an even number of days. The length of the mourning period depends on factors such as the age of the deceased and the social status. When people are mourning an unmarried person they remain in the area next to the entrance (ehale). The mourning ceremony for a wife takes place at the oshoto shondjugo (the assembly place near the hut for the wife). The mourning period for the husband takes place at the oshinyanga shamutyakemo (the assembly place of he who is not in). The mourning period for a child lasts for two days, the mourning period for a married person lasts for four days and the mourning period for a reigning chief may last for half a month.

The hair of orphans is shaved off during the mourning period. They wear black oshimona (traditional necklace). The widow is seated in the entrance and water is poured on her head.

The arriving women take their baskets off their heads and hold them up with their left hands above their left shoulders. The men rest their sticks on their left shoulders with their right hands. On this occasion the oshi- or ii is prefixed to every noun, e.g. oshinyama (bad meat), oshigumbo (bad house), to indicate that death is a dreadful thing.

A child is buried in the calf pen if it is a boy or in the oshini (pounding area) if it is a girl. A senior wife is buried in the elugo (kitchen) and junior wives are buried in the oshini. A married man is buried in the cattle kraal and the unmarried man is buried in the calf pen. A child that is born dead is buried in the urinal. The corpses of a person who dies during the mourning period of the reigning chief, criminals and unwanted children are not buried, they are just thrown into the bush.  According to Nghishiilenhapo (1996), the corpse of a person who fell from a tree is buried under the tree he/she fell off and the corpse of the person who drowned is buried near the water pond in which the deceased drowned.  The omukwaniilwa (the reigning chief) is buried in an ompampa (a cone made of poles on the grave of the king). A girl is ‘killed’ in order to accompany the omukwaniilwa. It is believed that such a girl has to give the omukwaniilwa water when he is thirsty. The subjects of the deceased omukwaniilwa are not allowed to work during the ongondji (the time of rest observed when the omukwaniilwa passes away).

When the mourners are leaving, they say: Twa ka yeeka or Twa piti mepya ( i.e. we are departing from death lamentation). A person that returns from the house of the deceased, does not pass by any house, because he/she does so, the people he /she talks to become contaminated by death. The other common expression uttered during the mourning period are Kund’ ongundu (greet the crowd), Ombidhi nayo omelila (The wild dog is also on its way), Olye a pumu kombago a dhimbuluthe omuteki (Who touches the water calabash to remind the one who has to collect water). During the mourning period of the chief the mourners say: Omo e li ? Ke mo. ( Is he in? He is not).

9. The Child Naming Ceremony

he father invites his relatives and friends who bring food and beer. A special friend of the father brings strands of bark from the omugolo1 (terminalia) bush and fashions it into small necklace and belt. With these he proceeds to the mother’s hut and places them on the child together with the customary leather made of oxhide. Sitting on the ground next to his wife the father takes up the infant and passed it under his legs twice before handing it back to her with the remark. Here is your child his /her name is so and so. The relatives and friends are then called and the name is disclosed. The child is given the oshinyenye2 (traditional necklace) at olupale lwondjugo3 (the sitting place opposite the mother’s hut).

The mother remains at the entrance of the hut with her child on her lap. She eats oshithima4 (mahangu porridge) and ekaka5 (dried spinach). She is then smeared with oil and the big morsel is dipped in the gravy which she eats greedily without breaking it into pieces. Every person in attendance rubs himself/herself with oil. A child is then given ondhikwa6 (a baby carrier) made from an animal skin which a particular clan admires. Someone from a paternal clan takes the child to the kraal if it is a boy and pretends to be looking after the cattle and tells the child how to look after them. If it is a girl it is taken to the pounding area where the pestle is taken and the person who carries the baby pretends to be pounding mahangu into flour (Estermann,1979); Tonjes, 1948; Mbenzi, 2000). The ceremony is accompanied by a popular dance:

Oshike she twe eta?

Oluvalo!

What has us made come?

Offspring!

This takes place either in the morning or in the afternoon. The mother sits opposite the ondjugo with the child on her lap. She eats a mahangu morsel which she accepts with both hands and swallows without chewing. The child is removed from her by the namer who holds it up and says the name. All the people in attendance rub themselves with the oil. They break into the popular dance: Oshike she twe eta? Oluvalo. (What has made us come here? Offspring.)

A paternal relative takes the child to oshini if it is a girl and pretends to be pounding mahangu into flour. He she takes the boy to the cattle kraal and introduces it to the cattle. The question asked after the birth of the baby if one wants to know the sex of the baby is: Iikwiila nenge omafuma? ( Is it mahangu cakes or frogs? ).  To say congratulations for your new born baby, one says: Ino ka fika. (Do not burn it).

10. The Oshipe (eating of the new product ceremony)

The Aawambo organize a thanksgiving ceremony after winnowing. The ceremony is organized to express thanks to the ancestors for the good harvest. Foods and drinks are prepared for the occasion. The hair of the house occupants is shaved off. Nobody is allowed to leave the house. Food and drink are taken to the place of ancestors for them to taste first. During the celebration, the husband takes a morsel of mahangu porridge and throws a morsel in each of the four directions. While he does this he says:

Yokuuzilo kwateni

Yokuuninginino  taambeni

Yokuumbugantu nane wo

Yokuumbangalantu osho wo.

(You of the east take.

You of the west take

You of the south also take

You of the north also take.)

A woman takes a cup and pours the beer into it until it overflows. The drink that sinks into the soil is believed to be consumed by the ancestors. The ceremony is accompanied by the shout of praise: Ilililililili by women or Uuwuh by men.

11 The Healing Ceremony

This ceremony is conducted in the afternoon. A dog or a goat is killed for a person with

minor ailment. The ox is slaughtered for the person with a serious ailment. The animal is simply strangled to death. It is believed that the sick is haunted by the maternal or paternal ancestors. The traditional healers slap the sick person on the head. The senior healer spits water from his/her mouth in the face of the ailing person. The other healers ask the ailing person:  Olye e ku na? or Olye e li mo?  Who has you? Or Who is in?. The sick person answers softly mentioning the name of one of the ancestors: Ongame ngandi (I am so and so).The next question, a healer asks is: Ngandi oto kongo shike mokaana? (So and so what are you looking for in the child?). The ailing person answers: Onda hala ongombe yandje. (I want my cow) The ailing person asks his relatives to provide a cow.

The next morning the blood of the animal is used to cast out the evil spirits. The nose of the dog is cut off and placed on a string of the pearls and the ailing person wears it around his/her neck. The tail of the goat with its fleece is placed on a string of pearls and the sick person wears it. The hair of the tail of the cow is plaited and placed around the neck of the ailing person. It may also be tied to the belt of the sick person. The ailing person takes the drink mixed with the herbs from the hands of the healer. The ailing person is cut and his/her blood is mixed with the blood of the animal. The mixture is given to the dog which consumes it. When the mixture is eaten up, it is a sign that the disease is removed from the sick person.

12. The Wedding Ceremony

The wedding party is a special occasion among the Aawambo. Before the fiancée and fiancé tie the knot, the hegona (a paternal relative) rubs them with oil mixed with the red ochre to bless them. Each of them is blessed by his/her hegona. On their day of their wedding they eat the ekaka (dry vegetable cake made from the omboga (fresh wild spinach) as a sign of good luck. (The ekaka is also eaten before one undertakes a trip to wish him/ her good luck.). On the day of the wedding, the hegona provides the bride and the bridegroom with the cup of omalovu (an alcoholic beverage). She pours the omalovu into oshitenga until it overflows. The omalovu that spills over are given to the ancestors to taste before the bride and bridegroom taste. The bride and the bride groom take trurns in receiving the oshitenga from the he-gona. The oshitenga is received with both hands. The bride or the bridegroom takes the omalovu without pausing to the last drop.

There are several sayings uttered during the wedding party in honour of the bride and the bridegroom. These are:

Wandu! Wandu! (It is obvious.)

Walakata! Walakata! (It is obvious.)

Tala kuulenga taa. (Look at the embellishments.)

Omwa tila! Omwa tila! (You are afraid.)

Nena otatu ithele (Today we are swaggering.)

Tala utale! Tala utale! (Just look.)

Apart from these general sayings, there are other expressions which introduce a particular ezimo (a clan) during the party, e.g.

Nayi shekete. Let it chirp. (for the locust clan)

Nena otali ulu. Today it is howling. (for the hyena clan)

Nali kookaye. Let it roll (for the snake clan)

Mushimba kutulula .The omushimba person unyoke.( for the cattle clan)

Oonakuyupa ka nik’ ontaku. Those with a small calabash that smells ontaku ( mahangu soft drink). ( For the corn clan)

Onime nuunimwena. The lions and cubs. (For the lion clan)

13 The Birth of Twins

Giving birth to twins is considered as a bad omen among the Aawambo. When a mother gives birth to twins, she is moved to a hut built in the mahangu field for her. She remains in that hut with her babies. She pounds mahangu into flour and prepares her food in that hut. When a passerby greets her, she responds by ululating. The father of the twins gives a shout of bloodguilt every morning.  He gives this shout while facing the east in the morning and in the evening she faces the west when he shouts. He shouts as follows: Wuu-wu, Wuu-wu. The mother and the babies do not clean themselves. The mother remains in tatters. She makes ugly baskets. A piece of clay pot in which the herbal mixture is kept, is placed at the entrance. Whoever wants to see the babies applies the herbal mixture to his/her wrist, ankles, the outer part of his /her hands and tongue.

There is a belief that if the mother of the twins is not purified, she gets swollen to death. On the day of the purification rite, two goats are slaughtered. One goat comes from the paternal clan and the other one comes from the maternal clan. A traditional healer identifies a place next to the omudhime bush where the rite takes place. A deep hole is dug and water is poured into this hole. Two sticks from the maternal clan and the other two sticks from paternal clan are thrown in the hole. The traditional healer mixes water with the herbs. The mother and father of the babies go into the hole naked. The relatives of the mother and father surround the hole. The bystanders are sprinkled with herbal mixture. When the mother and the father are out of the hole, the mother and the babies are smeared with the red ochre. In some cases the mother and all her children are seated in the hole and scratched with sharp objects all over their bodies by traditional healers

14. Moving to a New House

When the family moves into a new house, a paternal relative has to lead the procession. When they reach the intersection, they rest for a while. He/she gets up at dawn and leads her/his child to the new dwelling. The paternal relative, who leads the procession, carries the oshifonono from the mopane tree. He is followed by the owner of the new house who carries the calabash, bow and arrows, an assegai, a knobkerrie and a knife. The owner of the house is followed by the senior wife who carries her basket in which she keeps the gourd for oil and red ochre, the senior wife is followed by the junior wives who are followed by the children. When the procession arrives in the new dwelling, the people put their luggage at olupale where the paternal relative makes the fire to officially inaugurate the new dwelling. The owner of the house and his entourage warm themselves before the fire. The paternal relative puts the oifonono (fresh firewood) at the entrance of the house to bless the new dwelling.

15. Oshidhano shoongombe (Cattle dance/ Cattle festival)

There are several studies that focus on Omaludi eengobe. Tönjes (1911) describes a cattle festival that was held by King Uejulu yaHedimbi of Aakwanyama:

Every year there is a big cattle feast when the cattle from the different parts of the country are brought to the owner’ s kraal so that he, personally, can see if they have thrived and whether the herdsmen have taken good care of them. Once I was an observer at the cattle post of King Uejulu. Three or four thousand of his subjects formed a circle in a large open space. When the king appeared the various herdsmen drove the cattle that had been entrusted to them past him, with loud cries and shouts. At the same time the wildest kind of dance took place. The herdsmen greeted their ruler with  “Tatekulu, tala, tala inatu lifa nawa?” (Grandfather see, see have we not pastured cattle well?) Women surround especially fine animals and sang to them.

The description given by Tönjes requires an explanation. It does not specify the season of the year during which the festival takes place. Loeb (1962) explains that the cattle ceremony or oongobe tadi dane takes place in March or April, at the end of the rainy season. It is true that this festival take places at the end of April, after the rain has eased off. Fiina Jarius (as cited in Mbenzi, 2021), who lived in Epale, narrates that she observed Omaludi eengobe in Epale royal residence in Ondonga in April every year. Itamalo (2018), commenting on the Eengobe tadi dane festival in Oukadonaveendjeva village in Southern Angola notes that the shows are usually held at the end of autumn and the beginning of winter, mainly in the months of April and May. In most instances, neighbouring farmers and friends would have it each year, hosted by a different farmer on a rotational basis. Sometimes an affluent farmer would have it hosted who own large of cattle at their farm each year during the same period.

Several interviewees we spoke to confirmed that April was a suitable month for the festival because March was a rainy month hence its name Taula which means to wade through water. Thus it was impossible to host in March as Loeb alluded to.

In addition, Loeb does not mention the rituals that are conducted in honour of the living cattle nor does he describe how the best man is rewarded and the punishment meted out to the men with poorly fed cattle.

Estermann (1976) also describes the cattle ceremony in Uukwanyama during the reign of Mandume Ndemufao:

In pre-colonial times, one of the many ceremonies in Oukwanyama was the cattle festival, for which, after the rainy season, cattle owners brought their herds from the grazing outposts and were displayed to the community. This is however still practiced today, although not with the same vibrancy as in those days. Eengombe tadi dana cattle festival or cattle dance that was held at Omhedi or Ohaingu every year with dancing to the beat of drums was originally held at the royal palace at Ondjiva and Oihole before Mandume died in 1917 This was an annual event when cattle were brought back from the main areas of residence after grazing at outposts through the long dry season. Here, cattle owners would show off their fattest cattle and thus take part in the spectacle of wealth and power. There was a displacement (or redirection) of the festival from its previous royal location as it usually took place at the king’s palace “The big and medium proprietors customarily organize a festival every year to which they give the name okuludika eengobe, which may be translated as “to admire cattle.” He continues, “It is also called simply eengobe tadi dana (‘cattle dance’). This celebration never had, or does not now have, a very marked ritual character. The time at which it is celebrated is not stipulated  precisely and depends on the wish of the owner, but there is a preference for two seasons, the first after the eengongo marula fruit has been consumed and the second at the end of the harvest, when the cattle ‘go eat the straw in the fields.’

The description given by Estermann requires further explanations and comments. The aim of the Omaludi eengobe is not simply to show off the fattest cattle to the public, but to encourage cattle herders and cattle owners to increase productivity. The cattle owners and herders whose cattle do not win in the Omaludi eengobe get motivated to work harder to score high marks in the next competition and the cattle that are well taken care of  become productive as they produce enough milk and butter which serve as a supplement to the diet of the household members.

It has to be pointed out Omaludi eengobe is now practiced with vibrancy and is even recognized by modern authorities, for example, the Okongo Village Council hosts Omaludi eengobe every year from 1918 and “the  main idea is to try and create an opportunity for communal farmers to gain access to the market to sell and showcase their produce. This is also an opportunity for the role players in the agriculture industry to come and exhibit and present their products to the end users.” (Okongo to host Omaludi festival, p.1). This suggests that Omaludi eengobe is commercialised as the communal farmers are now given the opportunity to sell their cattle.

On the cattle festival discourse, Estermann (1976, p.113) narrates that:

On the day set, all the herdsmen must drive their herds into the corral show the cattle to the master. This applies only to the full-grown animals. They are all shut up in one or more corrals. On the next day, an hour more or less after sunrise, the herdsmen begin leading the cattle out. Meanwhile the chief wife of the owner is preparing a basket of ashes taken from the fireplace of the big courtyard. As the animals pass one by one through the narrow opening of the corral, she rubs the flank of each beast with the ashes she has prepared, and she and her companions utter the well-known shouts of joy, lililili…Then the herdsmen separate the cattle into little groups which they drive to the outer courtyard (oluvanda). There the animals, prodded by the herdsmen, take a few turns around the courtyard, while the owner and his invited friends make their observations on the cattle’s physical appearance. A negligent herdsman, whose cattle appear poorly fed, will be rubbed on the head and face with fresh cow droppings. After the dance, food and drink are served to the herdsmen and all the guests. The next day the owner of the cattle rewards the good herdsmen, giving each of them a heifer. Before the meeting breaks up he generally modifies the distribution of the cattle.

Here, Estermann is presenting a specific case of what happened during a cattle dance/festival. However, although some of the events he describes still take place today, most of them are not done anymore (Shiweda, 2011), for example the rubbing of negligent herdsman with fresh cow droppings.  The ash used by the chief wife requires an explanation. The ash in Oshiwambo is used to wish the cattle as well as people good luck. Furthermore, Estermann, however, does not describe eemhoko domaludi eengobe which is a part of cattle ceremony. Eemhoko domaludi eengobe is held the evening before the actual ceremony to welcome the herdsmen. On this evening the herdsmen show their talent and skill in singing the cattle songs. They also cracked jokes. Dettmann (2019), writing on the cattle songs of Ovanyaneka explains that:

regardless of whether they (cattle songs) are performed in solitude or as part of communal gatherings. The herder is constantly surrounded by cattle — as well as wildlife — and he can use the sound and visual stimulation of this experience as inspiration for the cultural expressions associated with pastoralism. Performing, or preparing a performance, constitutes a sustainable way of entertaining himself (p.52).

This suggests that cattle songs perform two main functions,  to entertain and inspire, but the aspects of praising cannot be underrated here because in cattle songs there are often references to clolour, walking, and physical appearance and the abilities of the animal being praised, for example an agile or nimble ox  is praised in songs for such traits.  Estermann, however, confirms the significance of cattle songs at cattle ceremony and gave an example of such a song. Estermann (1976) narrates that:

“it is natural that such songs are to be heard at a festival dedicated to the cattle, especially as these little verses set to music make up a part of the herdsmen’s life and in fact a part of the life of every man of the Owambo tribes. There is no young fellow who does not know a certain number of these songs or who does not sing them from time to time, when driving the cattle or when making butter. Even when doing work that has nothing to do with the cattle the men and boys are often heard singing pastoral songs.  These poems and songs are called eengovela. They are generally complete with proper names, names of big cattle owners and geographical terms used to indicate particular sites to which the herdsmen had driven the cattle for grazing. It ties herders and owners to the past and to landscapes with old and ongoing associations (p.98).

It is true that many young boys know how to sing cattle songs but there are currently some young men who cannot sing a verse of cattle song because of exposure to Christianity.  Some young men may sing religious songs while following cattle. Such songs have nothing to do with cattle, but cattle herders who care for cattle in ohambo are savvy cattle singers.

Loeb (1962) also gives detailed information about the celebration of cattle ceremony in Uukwanyama. He describes what happens on the evening prior to the day of the ceremony, giving information how the herdsmen are accosted by the chief wife and the second wife, role that the wife plays on this day and the songs which accompany the ceremony. This means that Loeb makes references to eemhoko deengobe. He further gives the role of the herdsmen, and the cattle owner. In addition, he mentions March or April as  months during which the celebration is held, but it must be pointed out that April or beginning of May are months appropriate for the cattle ceremony.  Loeb also describes in detail what happens on the day of the ceremony:

About ten the next morning the herdsmen bring cattle close to the kraal entrance. The kraal head appears dressed in his ceremonial front and back aprons. He dances up and down waving his spear and his bow and arrows. His second wife accompanies him, she dances her torso and paws the ground, holding her arms up like cattle horns; then lifts her skirts and shrills to the cattle. Carrying the branches of the life-giving mopane tree, the herds boys drive the cattle herd into the kraals. The head wife has the branches and the boys’ bows and arrows laid on her hut. With ashes from her fireplace and red ocher she sprinkles the backs of the cattle as the cattle enter the gate and marks the foreheads of the herd boys. First the young calves of the original cows enter the calf corral for inspection, the leanest cattle follow and sleekest come last. As herds boys drive the cattle into the corral, they blow their barks whistles and sing praises of the good care they have given their charges. The kraal owner replies with praise or blame as he sees it fit. According to his judgment at this time, he will allot each youth more or fewer cattle on the next trip to the outpost (pp.212-213).

However, Loeb does not mention the reward given to the best herdsmen. Furthermore he does not describe the rituals that accompany the ceremony. These rituals are conducted by both the herdsmen and the cattle owner and are believed to contribute to cattle winning in the ceremony Carrying branches of mopane tree symbolises good luck. Mopane is seen as magic tree in Oshiwambo thus its branches and sticks are used in benediction sessions. Like Estermann, Loeb stresses the significance of ashes in blessing cattle. The judges are not mentioned by Loeb. These judges look for certain qualities in the cattle and allocate marks accordingly. Loeb mentions cattle songs, he does not provide an example of cattle song.

Vamalimwe (as cited in Mbenzi, 2021) gives examples of a cattle song which are performed during the cattle ceremony, for example:

Omwa ufana nge  noneudo

Omwa ufana nge noneudo

Itayi kambele

Koluvanda latate

Oku na omhata

(You have invited even this year

You have invited even this year

I do not hesitate

There is competition

In my father’s cattle yard)

This is one of the songs sung at the beginning of the ceremony as the cattle herders arrive at the place of celebration but most of the songs were aimed at praising cattle, for example:

Ndongo ye tu londel’ okamwandi

Ye ka tya shikishiki

Manghanyena ina a pya

Fye ounona hatu li

(Ndongo climbed a small African ebony tree for us

It shook it

We, the children ate unripened African ebony fruit)

This song gives the name of an ox and the qualities of the ox are described through personification and flatteries. The ox is epitomized as animal which contributes to the diet of the children. The cattle praise by the cattle herders and cattle owners continue throughout the celebration.

16.Oshipe celebrations

There are several rituals and cultural norms that accompany oshipe shomahangu celebrations. Kreinath (2018) defines rituals as the orderly performance of a complex sequence of formulaic acts and utterances set apart from other forms of everyday activity through framing and formalization and of choreographed movements and gestures. In addition, Frese (n.d) defines cultural norms as “the shared perceptions of how people routinely behave in a culture.”

Some various cultural norms and rituals accompany oshipe shomahangu. Before the celebration of oshipe shomahangu, everyone is expected to be home. Mbenzi (2021) states that everyone from the homestead, where oshipe shomahangu is being celebrated, should be present during the celebrations. Tönjes (1996) adds that the husband summons all the household members as no one should be left out on this special celebration. If the children are out taking care of the cattle, they should be informed to attend the oshipe shomahangu celebration. Similarly, Vedder, Hahn, and Fourie (1928, as cited in Mbenzi, 2021) note that when the house owner celebrates oshipe, he invites all his wives and his children. This includes all his married children. When one marries or gets married, it means that they have a family and house of their own; but, in the Aawambo culture, one is still called to celebrate oshipe shomahangu at their father’s house. It appears that through oshipe shomahangu celebrations, family bonds are strengthened, and memories are created.

Before the actual celebration of oshipe shomahangu, Mbenzi (2021) notes that men shave their heads bald before the oshipe shomahangu celebration. Similarly, the Vambukushu, according to Tsoubaloko (2013), also get their hair cut for the new harvest ceremony. Specifically, Fumu, the chief of the Vambukushu, cuts his hair in preparation for the ceremony. Lastly, this information about cutting hair seems ancient, and this study seeks to establish whether Ondiikalela village residents still shave their heads bald. Furthermore, the cooking stick of oshipe shomahangu is always new. Nghishiilenhapo (1996, Ndeutapo,2014 & Mbenzi, 2021) state that oshipe shomahangu is cooked with a new stick either from the omulama or omusaati tree. Both of these trees, ‘omulama and omusaati’, are believed to be a source of luck, prosperity, and blessed life.

Furthermore, Nande (2015, as cited by Mbenzi, 2021) explains that “Munyalombe ota teleke oshithima noluko lwa za komupanda a taalela kuutokelo. Oshiti shika shomupanda ohashi longithwa opo aantu mboka ye li moshituthi ya kale ya panda.” (The first wife cooks the mahangu porridge using a new cooking stick, made from the ‘omupanda’ tree. The stem -panda means to be happy; thus, it is believed among Aawambo that the omupanda stick brings happiness. This cooking stick is used so that when the guests arrive, they become ecstatic during the harvest festival.)  It appears that the cooking sticks always have a significant role in the oshipe shomahangu celebrations.

It must be noted that all items to be used during oshipe shomahangu must be new. Mbenzi (2021) affirms that during oshipe shomahangu, everything used should be new, from the baskets, calabashes, omatemba (wooden sauceboat), omalilo (woven palm-leaf plate), and iitenga (wooden drinking cups) to the clothes worn by the housemates. New cooking stands also must be moulded. Using new items and containers signals the beginning of the new year and the end of the old year. This is encapsulated in the Oshiwambo apophthegm: Mumvo mukulu za mo mupe e ye mo (Old year go out, new one come in).

According to Mbenzi (2021), the day before the oshipe celebration, munyalombe (first wife), with the help of other women in the house, goes out to collect clay for the traditional cooking stands. Mbenzi (2021) elaborates that the day before the oshipe shomahangu celebrations, the first wife leads a few people of an even number. When collecting the clay soil, they do not greet anyone, nor do they respond to any greeting by anyone. However, Mbenzi does not explain why greeting was not allowed.  The clay purposely collected for the oshipe shomahangu is placed on the eastern side of oshinyanga as that’s the side where rain is made.

Furthermore, Mbenzi (2021) adds that the first wife then precedes by moulding the first traditional cooking stand, then the other wives mould their traditional cooking stands. The oshipe shomahangu traditional cooking stands are usually four. However, only three cooking stands are used, and the fourth one is a spare one used in case one of the three stands breaks.

Furthermore, on the day of oshipe shomahangu, food is cooked. The women also prepare traditional brews such as ontaku (soft meal brew) and omalovu giilya (alcoholic meal brew). Ndeutapo (2014) states that “efiku loshipe ovaneumbo ohava uhala nopiyepiye kokulongekida oikulya yoshivilo i kale ya wana, oifima nomalodu a kale mahapu” (on the day of oshipe shomahangu, family members get so busy preparing for the oshipe shomahangu ceremony, making sure that there is enough porridge and traditional brew).

Furthermore, Mbenzi (2021) states that the mixture of the traditional brew prepared at the house's main reception area is first poured onto the ground before the woman starts brewing omalovu giilya (traditional brew). This may be done so that the ancestors taste the people’s generosity.

Traditional food such as oshimbombo, ekaka, dry omboga, and oshigali are served on this day. According to Munyika (1997), dried spinach (ekaka) is believed to be connected to permanency, oneness, and fortune. It appears that Aawambo eat this traditional food because it is connected to their well-being as it is associated with luck. In addition, porridge is not served with meat as it is associated with death (Munyika, 1997). It appears that meat attracts bad luck into one’s life; thus, it cannot be consumed during an imperative occasion like oshipe. To do so would be to start the year with bad luck.

The food and meal-brew is taken to the senior wife’s section, in the living area, opposite the senior wife’s sleeping hut, where they eat (poshinyanga shondjugo) (Namibia Tentative National Inventory of ICH, 2019). 17. Epiitho (Outdoor ceremony)

When the infant is four days old, the epiitho (presentation of the child to relatives and intimate friends) takes place. The latter are invited to come to the kraal and partake of a light breakfast outside the hut in which the mother is. During the meal, the child is admired and fondled by all present and finally passed to an old male friend of the family. If the infant is a boy the old man hands him to his wife who takes him to the front entrance of the kraal to show him to the world. She then returns past the cattle enclosure to introduce him to the cattle, his main care. He is also taken and introduced to the milk calabashes and then returned to his mother. As h is handed to the latter all present clap their hands and exclaim that it is a boy who will grow to be a real man. In the case of a girl, a similar procedure is gone through with the exception that she is introduced to the threshing floor and the corn retainers instead of the cattle kraal and milk calabashes. Before the visitors depart the old woman gives the child a nickname by which it is known until formal naming by the father takes place about one month later.
