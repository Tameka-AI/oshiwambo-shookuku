# Aawambo Ethnobotany

15. Ethnobotany among Aawambo

1. Omugolo (terminalia  sericea)

Omugolo is a shrub that grows in the forests or plains. The name omugolo comes from the verb stem gooloka (literally, to return). The shrub is of cultural significance to the Aawambo. It is used to make the people recover from misfortunes or to wish them good luck.

Traditionally, when one is to undertake a long trip, one goes in the oshinyanga shondjugo (the reception area opposite the woman’s hut) to inhale the smoke. The woods of the omugolo are used in this case in the hope that when one inhales the smoke of the omugolo woods one would return safely. This wood is also used to bless men before they take part in the war. They go in the oshinyanga shogundjugo to inhale the smoke of the omugolo wood in the hope that they will not be killed in the war. When the soldiers or warriors are involved in the bush war, they have the day rest in the shade of the omugolo so that they can remain safe during the war.The hunters inhale the smoke so that they are safe during their hunting activities.

Omugolo keeps the family together. It strengthens the social contact between the neighbours and all members of the subtribe. Twigs of the omugolo are cut and strewn on the soil in the new house. This means happiness will reign in the new house. When people return from the long trip or fight, the question: Wa gooloka? is asked. This means one would like to stress that the person is back despite the difficult situation he/she has gone through. The same question may also be asked when someone absconded when he/she returns. In this case the question is used in the connotative sense, because such person is never blessed through inhaling of the smoke from the omugolo woods. The omugolo bush is planted in the new cattle pen, to ensure that many cattle will gather in the new cattle pen.

The omugolo is also used by Aawambo to treat certain ailments. When one suffers from gonorrhea or constipation, the leaves or barks of omugolo are taken to oshini   (pounding area) and ground to a pulp. The sick person drinks the pulp to get cured from the ailment. The roots of the omugolo are also used in the treatment of cough. One can chew the barks from the roots or the may be processed in oshini for the sick person to drink. The leaves of omugolo are used in the treatment of back pain. In the ancient time women who suffered from infertility were made to drink the concoction from the roots of omugolo   to make them fertile. When cattle have to go to the cattle post far away from the homestead, one of them may be hit one of them with a twig of omugolo so that they may not be attacked by wild animals. It is also believed that they will come back safely. There is a belief that when herdsman keeps a stick of  omugolo, his livestock cannot vanish and when they vanish they will simply return safely.

Omugolo produces resin. The resin is eaten by people especially men when they are tending livestock. The goats and cattle feed themselves on the leaves of omugolo. The games also browse on the leaves of the omugolo. The gum of omugolo is edible and may be used to glue the arrows.

Traditionally, children who have become frequent thieves are cured from theft with the omugolo woods. A parent may take a burning wood of omugolo and roast the fingers of a child. It is believed that such a child would stop stealing. Alternatively, a parent may put the hands of the child in the furnace of the omugolo woods. A child who passes urine in sleep is put on the omugolo. The person who puts it there cuts down the omugolo and leaves suddenly. He /she does not look back to see how it falls down.

The omugolo plays a significant role in the birth of a child. The nursing mother is massaged by the mid-wife with barks of omugolo. The rings of omugolo barks (iigodhi) are put around the neck, the loins, arms and the legs of baby before it is given a name. Two strands of the omugolo barks put around each of the body parts mentioned. This is an old tradition, but some people still practise it today.

Omugolo is important in the construction of homestead. The sticks of omugolo may be used to bless a new homestead by the he-gona (a father’s sister) of a man. She takes the four sticks and plants two sticks on each side of the entrance. The sticks of the omugolo may be used to construct a new homestead. Some men tie the sticks of omugolo into small bundles, taa dhingi iihwali. These bundles are planted into the soil in case there are no big sticks. The omugolo sticks are more resistant to mites attacks than sticks of other trees that may be readily available.  But it must be noted that the sticks of omuhongo, onyege, omuhama are more durable and stronger than that of omugolo but they are mostly available in big jungles (omithitu). The omithitu are situated far from many homesteads at present. The handle of the hand hoes are carved from omugolo.

2. Omusati (mopane tree)

The name of omusati is the truncated form of the name omusi a ti (the deceased once said). There is an anecdote about this tree. In the ancient times the Aawambo ancestors gave this tree a name. A after a few generations,  people forgot the name of the tree and began asking one another’ “What did the deceased say?’ In other words, What name did the ancestors give to this tree?  People could not remember the name of the tree, therefore they started calling it omusaati (the deceased once said).

Omusati plays a significant role in the life of the Aawambo. It is a magic tree. The leaves of the omusati are strewn in the new dwelling to wish the house dwellers good luck. A paternal relative of the house owner throws the leaves around the new dwelling and put some leaves on the pole of the homestead. As he she does this, he/she says a prayer: May house be filled with peace and harmony. Some paternal relatives throw the leaves around without saying a word. Twigs are chopped from omusati and thrown into every hole dug during oludhilu (when a new house is erected. The twigs of omusati used for appeasing hatred.

When one person migrates from one tribal area into another, the leaves of omusati are strewn on the ground and the migrants tread on them, taa lyata iifo. Treating on the leaves of omusati means the migrant remains protected and the people he/she will meet will give him/her a warm welcome. They are placed on the road offering fortune to a traveller; that he will be received well and get good field The mopane fruit are used in weather forecast. It is believed that when too many omakoti appear on the omusati, there will be good showers in that year. The fresh sticks of omusati are used in blessing the new dwelling. A paternal relative brings along two or four six sticks of omusati and heat them in the fire. The new occupants of the dwelling are expected to inhale the smoke of the iithonono (fresh sticks). The omusati can be used a firewoods. Some people prefer the firewoods of omusati to the firewoods of omugolo. They put log the main reception area (oshinyanga oshinene). The log of omusati keeps burning to wish the family good luck.

The omusati plays a role in moving the new homestead. The first work related  to moving a home is to fetch bark slabs from the woods, omihuya, to be used as binds and to erect the omusindilo (magic stick) and oshinangelamwali ( a type of grass which  magically allows one to remain longer in a place). Small fresh omusati trees are chopped down and their barks are taken off. They are bound into balls. In the middle of the ball, a small twig from the omusati tree is put. Three chops of fresh omusati wood and twigs from liana are brought in the living room. The sauce pot is placed on these chops and when it is ready and porridge is cooked upon the same fire.

The sticks of omusati are used as building materials. The barks of the omusati are used in making the roof of the traditional huts. The wooden hoops are fastened with the barks and the omatsatsa (wooden hoops) are made with oombala (mopane woods after the barks have been removed) from the omusati. Pounding blocks are carved from omusati as well as the pestles.

The mopane worms browse on the leaves of the omusati. Omagungu are delicious food for the Aawambo and are harvested for commercial purposes.  Before the rainy season the mopane trees become the living place for omambulunganga (edible insects). The omambulunganga are harvested and cooked for meal. The omusati also produce uutushi (sugary secretion on mopane leaves). The uutushi are collected and eaten, particularly by the children.

In modern times, the omusati is important during Christmas time. The house owners cut the mopane tree and plant them in their homesteads to symbolize the birth of Christ. The twigs are put on the poles during this time. It is not known why Christians have choses the omusati as a Christmas tree. But it can be assumed that its magical significance have encouraged Christians to use it. In Oshiwambo this is tree of happiness and since Christ mas is the time of joy, this tree befits the occasion. On new year eve Christians put fresh omisati  in their homestead to welcome the dawn of the new year.

3. The ekaka and omboga  as a symbol of happiness , unity and progress

Omboga is type of wild spinach which grows in mahangu field or in the field that has remained fallow for years. The Aawambo rejoice when they see omboga grown so well in a field. They believe that when they have omboga then they eat their oshithima  with relish. Omboga drives away lack of sauce, hayi tidha po omukaga. Different types of oomboga (plural of omboga) grow in the field such as omboga yompungu (spinach made of pedicellaria pentaphylla, omboga yoontaga (spinach made of pumpkin leaves, omboga yomakunde (spinach made of bean leaves, omboga yanakwadhigwa (mushed spinach), omboga yelopa, spinach stew made of pumpkin. Sometimes omboga may be boiled and stirred with a stick (oludhigo) so that it becomes soft enough to dip a mahangu morsel into when eating.

The Aawambo welcome the dawn of the New Year by throwing a party in honour omboga.  This party is called oshipe shomboga (eating the fresh omboga).  The woman picks the leaves in the field, ta mu omboga. Omboga is cooked in a clay pot at the reception area opposite the wife’ shut, poshinyanga shondjugo). Omusati woods are used to cook omboga because omusati is magical tree for luck and good wishes. Omboga is mixed with omundjulu (edible spinach, seseuvium sesuvioides or ekwakwa (kind of plant,chenopodium) to add good taste to it. The householders apply the omboga to their navel. A fresh mopane wood remains on fire producing a smoke which the house occupants inhale symbolizing, the dawn of the New Year. The housemaster throws morsel in the west and east while saying: Mumvo mukulu za mo omupe e ye mo (Old year go out so that the New Year may come in). When people meet in the New Year or when they meet after they have not seen each other for a long time, they say: Twa lile ompungu tu mu mone omboga yanakwadhigwa tu mu tye meho (We have not seen him since we ate the ompungu, we have not seen him since we ate the mushed omboga).

All members of the family are expected to imitate the husband. The husband then eats the omboga together with ekala, a piece of the coal from the fire just lit.  The ekala symbolizes good luck. The ekala is also used to drive away bad spirit and misfortunes. One is not allowed to eat omboga before the offering to the ancestral spirit is made. Should one break this taboo, it is believed that he/she will suffer from an incurable stomachache.

Ekaka is a dry vegetable cake made from omboga (wild spinach). The omboga is boiled in a pot. When it is ready, it is put in the sun for a while to drain it. The omboga is then pounded in the oshini to soften it. A woman takes a piece of cooked omboga and presses it between her hands to make ekaka. Her hands leave marks of five fingers. This is why it is nicknamed antano (five). Ekaka is eulogised as Indongo yokiithima (the black one for the porridge), because it is often served with oshithima.

Ekaka performs various functions in Oshiwambo tradition. One has to eat it before he/she undergoes a  long trip. It is believed that ekaka has some magic power to protect a traveler. A married woman always keeps ekaka in a pot with mahangu flour to ensure that her family members do not fall prey to hunger wherever they are. At wedding party eating ekaka is obligatory. The bride and the bridegroom have to eat ekaka so that they remained united throughout their marriage life. It is believed that if the married couple refuses to eat ekaka on their wedding day their marriage life will be characterized by endless squabbles and conflicts. Several Aawambo eulogise ekaka as Ekaka tali kakele kumwe; evanda tali vandalaleke (ekaka unites [people]; evanda makes one bend to avoid dangers.)

Ekaka is eaten by the future son- or daughter -in- law on the he/ she is introduced to future parents in laws. A future son or daughter- in- law is not allowed to eat chicken, and goat’s meat or fish as eating these meats predicts bad omen. Eating ekaka and oshithima is believed to ensure that the couple will not break up and that happiness will reign in their future life. Out of ignorance and obliviousness,   several Aawambo, at present, eat  kinds of foods which are forbidden at etseyiko, introducing the future daughter or son- in-law to the future parents- in- law . Ekaka is used to demonstrate whether a future in law is approved or not. After  the future- in- law is cross-examined by the panel of interviewers (relatives and some neighbours), the mother of the future in-law, puts either the even number of the omakaka (plural of ekaka) or odd number of the omakaka in a basket and hands the basket  back to a woman who has accompanied the future in- law.  A member of an entourage, a woman always brings the basket with the flower in. If one or three omakaka place in a basket, it means the future in-law is not approved.
