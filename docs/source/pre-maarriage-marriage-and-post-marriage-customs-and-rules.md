# pre-maarriage, marriage and post marriage customs and rules

#### Pre-marriage arrangement, Marriage customs, and post-marriage customs

Aawambo are generally exogamists. In other words, intermarriage is forbidden between people from the same totemic clan. For example, a person from the Aakwanambwa clan may not marry a person of the same clan, even if they may be of a different ethnic group. This is based on the common understanding that any person belonging to one’s clan, whether they are from the same or different community, society, nation, country or continent, is considered a relative and therefore intermarriage is prohibited.

Among Ovambadja, Ovandombodola, Aakwambi and Aambalantu cross cousin marriages are also preferred since they “...keep the cattle from going away” (Estermann 1976, p. 93). Unions between mother’s brother’s daughter and mother’s daughter’s son are acceptable, as the mother’s brother’s daughter is not a member of the mother’s brother’s matrilineage or clan, but of the mother’s brother’s wife. Despite this, “the Ovakwanyama do not readily condone cross-cousin marriage on the mother’s side because the protagonists are classificatory siblings” (Loeb 1948, p. 20). Such marriages do occur among Aandonga, but are not nearly so popular as the cross cousin marriages on the father’s side (Tuupainen, 1970, p. 38).

Tuupainen (1970) explains the difference between preferential marriages and prohibited marriages. She stresses that in anthropology, preferential marriage is seen as marriage that promotes the existing system or strengthening it while prohibited marriage as marriage that causes disorder in the established system. Tuupainen further lists types of preferential and prohibited marriages respectively:

#### Preferential marriages

Father’s sister’s daughter

Mother’s brother’s daughter

Cross cousin: father’s sister’s daughter and mother’s brother’s daughter

#### Prohibited marriage

Mother’s sister (except father’s sister’s son’s daughters), daughters (except fathers mother’s brother’s daughter) maternal niece

Parallel cousin: mother’s sister’s daughter/ brother’s daughter

Wife’s mother

Son’s wife

Sister’s husband (marriage exchange)

Grand-daughter marriage (son’s daughter and daughter’s daughter)

Wife’s brother’s daughter

Wife’s sister (sororal polygyny or sororate) (p.102)

Tuupainen’s list does not wholly apply to Aawambo marriage, for example, Aawambo traditionally allows sororate and levirate marriage. One may marry his wife’s sister.

Marriage is considered a contract between two families. Thus, a boy and a girl have no right to start arrangements for marriage without the consent of their parents or guardians. The bridal price (iigonda) is determined by the parents of the girl. In other words, pre-and post-marriage rules are introduced by the parents of the bride and bridegroom. However, the role of the families in contracting marriages is on the wane.

According to Tuupainen (1970), the bridegroom seeks the consent of the girl’s parents for engagement. The boy receives permission from his father to engage. The father may refuse to grant permission if the girl hails from a clan with qualities that the father deems unsuitable for marriage. The girl’s family may also refuse to consent for the engagement for the same reasons. The prospective bridegroom is formally introduced to the family of the prospective bride (etseyiko). On the day of etseyiko a man or a woman is asked

to reveal both his paternal and maternal clan. The question is asked: Ezimo lyeni olini? (To what clan do you belong? A further question may be asked, “Kootate omukwashike? (What is your paternal clan?). The names of the clans are asked to ensure that the woman and man are not related by consanguinity because such a union is prohibited and seen as an incestuous relationship.

It must be noted that etseyiko involves the clansmen of the bride or the bridegroom who may either give a go-ahead or oppose the proposed union. After this, the prospective bride goes through the same procedure. Once the agreement between the two families has been sealed, each family informs its clan leader. After this, enongono (the period during which the biological parents give their relatives a tip-off before the official wedding invitations) commences.

During the celebrations, the clansmen praise themselves in honour of their clans; they imitate the sounds of their totems if their totems are animals, for example, members of the Aakwanekamba clan howl like a hyena and jokingly warn the attendants: “Kwateni uukombo weni nawa otse mboka tatu zi hoka.” Take care of your goats carefully we are on the way. This is said because the hyena is known for attacking goats. Members of the Aakwaniilya clan may hold small baskets containing millets or sorghum and throw the seeds around showing their presence. The Aakwanangombe clansmen utter the sound: Buu, Buu imitating the sound of cattle. The Aakuuta clan members carry the tree stumps on their backs during the weddings and throw ooshegele (raisin bush fruits) around, because it is believed that that is their fruit. The dancing acts may also reveal the clan the people belong to.

In some cases, the dress may tell maternal and paternal relatives of the bride or bridegroom, though this is very rare.

Members of a particular clan see it their exclusive right to wear dresses symbolising their totems. They may feel offended if a non-member wears a dress sporting their clan emblem. Wearing t-shirts and headdresses sporting clan emblem is becoming a common occurrence during the wedding ceremony among Aawambo as is evident in the preceding picture.

The dancing and dress of one particular clan makes another clan unhappy if copied. A question may be asked, “Ngweye Omukwanekamba sho wa zala okambindja ke na embungu? Why do you sport a t-shirt with a hyena as if you belong to Aakwanekamba clan? Frazer (1910) observes that the assimilation of men to their totems occurs through dressing in the skin or feathers of the totem, dressing in the hair of a totem, knocking out teeth or chipping teeth to imitate certain animals. Totems may also be painted on bodies,

weapons, boats, etc. Similar behaviour is often exhibited during the annual Oshakati Totem Expo in Namibia where individuals sport dresses symbolizing their totems.

In addition to objects, that identifies a specific clan, Aawambo clans have clan necklaces, designed to represent a specific clan. One can identify a persons’s clan, through the necklaces. Mwanyangapo (2017) describes different traditional necklaces that represent certain Aawambo clans. It is mostly children that wear these necklaces given to them by their paternal clan.

Furthermore, Estermann (1976) stresses that following marriage, a woman goes to live with her husband in the egumbo (homestead) of his family. She spends about two to three years in her husband’s home, residing in independent living quarters and managing her grain fields. Although she becomes part of her husband’s family, she does not abandon her clan, its customs and practices. She continues to uphold the taboos of her clan. If she is to conceive any children, she will teach them the “dos” and the “don’ts” of her clan. However, her children will also observe their father’s clan taboos. Loeb (1962) explains the post-wedding customs of the Ovakwanyama:

Under the rules of patrilocality or avunculocality a bride leaves her father’s or her maternal uncle’s kraal at the time of her marriage. A young groom with his wife remains in his father’s kraal or his maternal uncle’s kraal until he obtains a kraal of his own. Either the couple will be given a secluded epata (a living space in the kraal with its huts and equipment) or else the groom’s mother, his mother’s sister or some other near relative will give part of her epata to the bride temporarily (p.129).

The epata is given to the bride by her mother-in-law because the mother-in-law is in charge of epata. Epata is the department of a woman in the homestead. A woman will be unhappy if she is not given epata by her mother-in-law. The giving of epata to the bride is in some cases called okupewa omasiga (the giving of hearthstones). The occasion signals the legalization of marriage in Oshiwambo tradition. If the mother-in-law fails to give her daughter -in- law omasiga after a year, then the ‘bride’ believes that she is given cold shoulder. This tendency is still common among Aawambo today. A woman may even leave the homestead of his father’s or mother’s in-law if the giving of omasiga is delayed. Expressions such as omukiintu elugo (A woman is equal to epata) or Omukiintu omasiga (A woman is equal to omasiga) show that a woman without omasiga or elugo is the same as a woman who is not married at all, because if she dies during this period, the mourning period will take place in the homestead of her parents.

Besides, it must be noted that matrilocality is also practiced by Aawambo. This is confined to royal people only. A royal woman chooses her future husband. Once they are married the groom lives in the royal homestead of the bride’s parents. This practice continues among Aawambo. Even if they move out of the royal homestead to establish their new homestead, they often establish their homestead near or in the vicinity of the royal homestead. But modernisation has an impact on this trend because those who choose to live in urban centres are forced to erect their houses far away from the royal residence.

During the early days of marriage, it is quite common for women to return to their natal epata, to spend time with members of their lineages, and especially to give birth. The underlying principle behind this is that the new mother will receive all the necessary support from her experienced mother. During this period, the new mother is not able to cook. Thus, her mother takes care of her and assists her in nursing the baby. In modern times, several young women do not return to their natal epata after giving birth. However, their mothers make all efforts to come and live with them for a certain period.

Williams (1988) asserts that:

After about three years, the couple is usually able to establish an egumbo of their own. However, if the husband is the only son or the youngest, then the couple will live permanently with his matrilineal family. Omagumbo are not grouped into a ‘village’ as such but are scattered throughout a demarcated area known as an omukunda (district). Each omukunda contains on average 15-20 omagumbo, with the distance between each egumbo varying between 500m and 3km (pp. 46-52).

However, Williams does not reveal whether Aawambo follow a patrilocal or matrilocal residence pattern. Aawambo follow the patrilocal residence pattern because a woman joins the family of her husband. Furthermore, they establish their new egumbo mostly in the vicinity of the husband’s kin. There are, however, a few cases where the new couple has established their homestead near the homestead of the woman’s parents. In this case, it is said: Omusamane okwa tulwa mo (The husband is henpecked).

Writing on the place of residence among the Ovakwanyama, Bruwer (1966) argues that:

No basic principles are governing the place of residence as far as adult males are concerned. It is a matter of personal preference, and a man may erect his village in any ward or any district anywhere in Uukwanyama, provided he can acquire the necessary land. There is no kinship, lineal, clan or administrative principles regulating place of abode. Since the pattern of residence is neolocal, wives and children stay in the village of their husbandand father. There is naturally sometimes the ten dency for close relatives to erect their villages in adjacent vicinity, but this is by no means regulated by strict principles (p. 41).

Bruwer’s observation is correct to a certain extent, but it cannot go unchallenged. Strict principles are regulating the residence of a new couple in their first year of marriage. All Aawambo practice patrilocal residence in the initial year or years of marriage and afterward neolocal residence rule may be applied. There is no way new couple can move in their new residence without staying temporarily in the homestead of the parents of the husband. Of course, neolocal residence is not regulated but the new couple is encouraged to erect their homestead in the proximity of homestead of the husband’s parents. This practice has virtually become impossible due to population explosion.
